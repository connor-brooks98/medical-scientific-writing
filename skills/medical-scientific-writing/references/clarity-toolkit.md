# Clarity toolkit

Sentence- and paragraph-level craft for scientific prose, mainly from Kristin Sainani's *Writing in the Sciences* (Stanford), with reader-clarity rules from medical editing and a plain-English mode adapted from ASD-STE100. Use this when revising for concision and readability, or when a draft is clean of AI patterns but still hard to read.

## 1. Principles

Good scientific writing communicates an idea clearly and efficiently; elegance is secondary. The core moves:

- Cut unnecessary words and phrases.
- Prefer the active voice where the actor matters.
- Use strong, specific verbs; don't turn verbs into nouns.
- Keep the subject and main verb close together, near the start.
- Put one idea in each paragraph, and give the point early.

Verbs move a sentence along; nouns slow it down.

## 2. Cutting clutter

Kinds of clutter:
- **Dead-weight phrases**: as it is well known, as it has been shown, it can be regarded that, it should be emphasized that, it is noteworthy that.
- **Empty words**: important, basic tenets of, methodologic, various, very, really, quite, basically, generally.
- **Redundant pairs**: successful solution, completely eliminate, future plans, currently underway, unexpected surprise, studies/examples, illustrate/demonstrate.
- **Long words where short ones work**: utilize (use), facilitate (help), in close proximity to (near), commence (start).
- **Unnecessary jargon and acronyms.**
- **Negatives that can be positives**: "did not remember" (forgot), "not harmful" (safe), "does not have" (lacks), "did not succeed" (failed).
- **"There is / there are" openings**: "There was a reduction in mortality" (Mortality decreased). "The data confirm that there is an association" (The data confirm an association).

| a majority of | most |
| a number of | several, many (or the number) |

| give rise to | cause |
| has the ability to | can |

## 3. Verbs

- **Ask "who does what to whom?"** to recover the active form. "A recommendation was made by the DSMB that the study be halted" becomes "The DSMB recommended halting the study."
- **"We" is honest.** It claims responsibility for a decision or assertion. Avoiding it does not make science more objective.
- **Passive voice sparingly and purposefully**: in Methods when the procedure matters more than the actor, or when the actor is unknown.
- **Resurrect smothered verbs**: obtain estimates of (estimate), has seen an expansion in (expanded), offer confirmation of (confirm).
- **Choose the verb that carries the adverb**: "reports approximately... and estimates that... will double" becomes "estimates... and projects...".
- **Use "to be" verbs sparingly**, but don't contort sentences to avoid them; copula avoidance ("serves as") is worse (pattern S2).
- **Grammar points**: "data" is plural; affect (verb) vs effect (noun); "compared with" for differences between like things, "compared to" for likening unlike things; "that" restrictive, "which" non-restrictive (with commas).

## 4. Sentences

- **Don't bury the main verb.** If more than about 12 words separate subject and verb, restructure. "One study of 930 adults with MS receiving care in two managed-care settings found that..." becomes "One study found that, among 930 adults with MS in two managed-care settings, ...".
- **Vary length and structure** (pattern S8). Long sentences carry qualification and context; short ones state claims. Let content decide.
- **One comparison or condition per sentence** when the reader must hold several in mind.
- **Avoid noun stacks** of more than three nouns; unpack them into phrases that show how the parts relate.

## 5. Punctuation

In increasing power to separate: comma, colon, dash, parentheses, semicolon, period. In increasing formality: dash, parentheses, then comma, colon, semicolon.

- **Semicolon**: joins two closely related independent clauses; separates list items that contain internal commas.
- **Colon**: introduces a list, explanation, or amplification; can join two clauses when the second extends the first ("The concentration was set by intensity: if a scent is too strong, people rate it unfavorably.").
- **Parentheses**: non-essential asides and abbreviations; the sentence must still work without them.
- **Dash**: Sainani teaches the em dash as a legitimate tool for emphasis, used sparingly. This skill's default nonetheless replaces em dashes in manuscript prose, because their frequency is now one of the most recognized markers of machine-generated text and several medical style sources ban them outright. Use a colon for emphasis, parentheses for an aside, or commas for an apposition. Follow the author's or journal's house style if it uses them.

## 6. Parallelism

Pairs joined by and/or/but, and items in a list, take the same grammatical form.

- "This research has four phases: (1) establishing measurement instruments, (2) pattern measurement, (3) developing interventions, (4) the dissemination of..." becomes "(1) establishing..., (2) measuring..., (3) developing..., (4) disseminating...".
- "The velocity decreased by 50% but the pressure decreased by 10%" (parallel).

## 7. Paragraphs and organization

- One paragraph, one idea. Give the point early; the first and last sentences are the ones readers remember.
- Order ideas logically: chronological, general to specific, or premise to conclusion.
- Flow comes from logic and from echoing key terms, not from transition words. Transition words cannot fix logic that is not there. Use them where a real relation needs signaling (contrast, consequence, concession), not as decoration.
- Group like ideas and like paragraphs. In arguments, present the supporting points, then the counter-arguments, then the rebuttals, rather than alternating sentence by sentence.
- Paragraph revision method: write a one-line summary of each sentence (an outline), delete duplicates, move misplaced ideas, then rewrite from the outline. Sainani's worked examples routinely cut paragraphs from about 200 words to about 90 with no loss of content.
- Some information is clearer as a table, flow diagram, or tree than as prose.

## 8. Repetition, keywords, acronyms

- Before reaching for a synonym, ask whether the second instance is needed at all, and whether a synonym is really better than repeating the word.
- **Keywords must be repeated exactly.** Changing a term signals a new concept (pattern S5).
- Don't abbreviate a term just because it recurs. Use standard abbreviations (DNA, MRI); define others at first use in the Abstract, again in the main text, and in each table or figure legend; for long papers, redefine occasionally.

## 9. Reader clarity before compression

Compressed prose omits an actor, object, comparator, condition, or logical relation that the reader must reconstruct. It is a clarity defect even when the sentence is short and grammatical. Write for expert readers who may not be native English speakers.

Diagnostics:
1. **Name what an abstract verb means.** "The intervention adds value beyond usual care" (which outcome? compared with what?).
2. **Give pronouns unambiguous referents.** "The groups differed in age and treatment duration. This explained the difference." (What explained what? Was there an adjusted analysis?)
3. **Separate stacked conditions.** "With matched inputs and fixed thresholds, A outperformed B under default settings at lower cost" packs four conditions into one modifier structure.
4. **Turn noun chains into actions** (pattern S9).
5. **Distinguish retrospective evaluation from prospective use.** A threshold chosen after seeing the labels does not show users can choose it prospectively.
6. **State a connection instead of announcing one.** "Performance also changed. The larger batch was faster but less sensitive." becomes one sentence.
7. **Don't summarize every paragraph** (pattern D9). Keep interpretation that connects findings to prior evidence; delete restatement.
8. **Keep Abstract methods and results aligned.**

Final audit questions:
- Can the reader identify actor, object, comparator, and conditions without guessing?
- Does each sentence add a finding, a necessary condition, an interpretation, or a needed connection?
- Did shortening preserve the correspondence between methods and results?
- Did edits stay within the requested scope? (A request to tighten the Discussion does not authorize changes to Methods.)

## 10. Plain-English mode (STE-inspired)

For lay summaries, patient materials, consent documents, protocols, and step-by-step instructions, apply a light version of ASD-STE100 Simplified Technical English (Issue 9, 2025). This is a style target, not a compliance claim; strict STE compliance requires the official dictionary.

1. Identify the reader's question and the facts that answer it. Give the main idea first.
2. Use common words, concrete subjects, and active verbs. Define necessary terms once.
3. One name per concept; no decorative synonyms.
4. Descriptive sentences 25 words or fewer; instructions 20 words or fewer; one instruction per sentence; imperative verbs.
5. Put a condition before the action: "If you feel dizzy, sit down." not "Sit down if you feel dizzy."
6. One topic per paragraph; at most about six sentences.
7. Break noun groups longer than three words into phrases.
8. Preserve facts, uncertainty, units, exceptions, and safety information exactly.

Before: "It is imperative that patients ensure that the medication is administered prior to the consumption of food."
After: "Take the medicine before you eat."

## 11. The writing process

Roughly 70% pre-writing, 10% first draft, 20% revision.

- **Pre-writing**: gather and organize facts and citations before drafting; work out the take-home messages; build an outline by paragraph. Don't gather and write at the same time.
- **First draft**: write quickly in complete sentences, in order, focusing on logic over polish.
- **Revision**: read aloud; do a verb check (underline main verbs; look for "to be", passive, buried verbs); cut clutter; tag each paragraph with its main point and reorder; get a reader outside the subfield to identify the main finding and point to hard passages.
- **Order for an original manuscript**: tables and figures, Results, Methods, Introduction, Discussion, Abstract.

## 12. Final consistency checks

- **Internal consistency**: Methods says follow-up was at least 2 years; Results reports a mean of 1.5 years? Fix or explain.
- **Numerical consistency**: Abstract, text, tables, and figures agree; n in the Abstract matches Table 1; percentages match their numerators and denominators.
- **References**: no "reference to nowhere" (citation doesn't support the claim), no misinterpretation or exaggeration of the cited finding, primary rather than secondary sources for statistics, correct numbering and placement.
