# Draft manuscript sections: PPI use and 90-day CDI after pneumonia admission

Target journal: JAMA Network Open (retrospective cohort). Bracketed items need your input before submission.

---

## Results (primary and secondary outcomes)

Of 6,214 adults aged 65 years or older admitted with community-acquired pneumonia between 2016 and 2023, 2,377 (38.3%) were taking a PPI at admission and 3,837 (61.7%) were not. Within 90 days of discharge, C difficile infection (CDI) occurred in 4.1% of PPI users compared with 2.6% of non-users (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR], 1.48; 95% CI, 1.12-1.96). Ninety-day all-cause mortality was 11.8% among PPI users and 11.2% among non-users (aHR, 1.03; 95% CI, 0.88-1.21). Thirty-day readmission occurred in 17.9% and 16.4%, respectively (aHR, 1.06; 95% CI, 0.94-1.20). In the prespecified sex subgroups, the estimated association was 1.81 (95% CI, 1.24-2.64) in men and 1.21 (95% CI, 0.81-1.80) in women, with no evidence that the association differed by sex (P for interaction = .14). ## Discussion, paragraph 1 (principal finding)

In this retrospective cohort of older adults hospitalized with pneumonia, home PPI use at admission was associated with a 48% higher hazard of CDI over the following 90 days, with 4.1% of PPI users developing CDI compared with 2.6% of non-users. This association was not accompanied by differences in 90-day mortality or 30-day readmission, which suggests that the excess CDI burden was not a marker of a general increase in adverse outcomes in this population. The magnitude of our estimate is consistent with the pooled estimate from a 2012 meta-analysis of observational studies, which found that PPI use was associated with incident CDI (pooled odds ratio, 1.74).^1 That meta-analysis pooled mostly case-control and unadjusted studies, whereas our estimate comes from a single-system cohort with adjustment for age, sex, comorbidity, recent hospitalization, and index-stay antibiotic class. Our findings therefore add a contemporary, adjusted estimate from an older adult pneumonia population, a group in whom CDI carries substantial morbidity and in whom acid suppression is common.

## Discussion, paragraph 2 (sex differences and inpatient evidence)

The point estimate was larger in men than in women, but the interaction test was not significant (P = .14) and the confidence intervals overlap widely. We therefore interpret the sex-stratified estimates as hypothesis-generating rather than as evidence of effect modification. Biologically plausible explanations, such as sex differences in gut microbiota or in acid suppression pharmacology, have not been tested in this dataset and should not be inferred from these results. The inpatient literature on PPIs and CDI is also mixed. In a retrospective cohort of hospitalized patients with incident CDI, concurrent PPI receipt was not associated with recurrence (hazard ratio, 0.82; 95% CI, 0.58-1.16).^2 That study addressed a different outcome, recurrence among patients already infected, so it does not contradict our finding of an association with incident CDI. It does suggest that the effect of acid suppression may differ by clinical context, and that the timing of exposure relative to infection and hospital care matters. Future work should examine whether the association varies by PPI exposure duration, by in-hospital PPI initiation, and by concurrent antibiotic exposure, which prior work has suggested may amplify risk.^1

## Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the design is retrospective and single-system, which limits generalizability and leaves residual confounding. Pneumonia severity measures, such as the CURB-65 or Pneumonia Severity Index, were not available, and PPI prescribing may reflect illness severity or other indications, so confounding by indication is likely. Length of stay and intensive care use were not adjusted for, and longer stays increase both hospital antibiotic exposure and opportunity for CDI. Second, PPI exposure was ascertained only at admission from medication reconciliation. We do not know duration, dose, adherence, or whether PPIs were continued or started during hospitalization or at discharge. Patients who started a PPI during admission would have been classified as non-users, which would likely bias the association toward the null, but the direction of bias for other exposure misclassification is uncertain. Third, CDI was identified only through laboratory testing within our health system. Patients who were tested or treated outside the system, or who died before testing, may have been missed, and outcome ascertainment may differ by exposure group. Fourth, death within 90 days is a competing risk for CDI. Because the Cox model treats death as censoring, the hazard ratio is not the same as the cumulative incidence and should be interpreted accordingly; [add a sensitivity analysis with a cause-specific or Fine-Gray model if available]. Fifth, 312 patients (5.0%) were excluded for missing medication reconciliation. If missingness was related to exposure or outcome, selection bias is possible. Sixth, the sex-stratified analyses were prespecified but underpowered, and the interaction test was not significant. Finally, the 2016-2023 study period spans changes in antibiotic and PPI prescribing, including during the COVID-19 pandemic, which could affect both exposure and CDI rates.
