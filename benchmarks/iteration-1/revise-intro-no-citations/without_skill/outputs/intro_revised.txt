Introduction

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is the most common sustained cardiac arrhythmia in adults and a leading cause of cardioembolic stroke [REF]. Its prevalence rises steeply with age, and adults aged 75 years and older account for a disproportionate share of both prevalent AF and AF-related stroke [REF]. Because AF is often asymptomatic or paroxysmal, many cases remain undiagnosed until a complication occurs [REF]. European guidelines recommend that screening be considered in individuals aged 75 years or older [REF], whereas the US Preventive Services Task Force has concluded that current evidence is insufficient to assess the balance of benefits and harms of screening [REF].

Consumer smartwatches offer a scalable means of rhythm assessment. Most combine photoplethysmography (PPG), which passively assesses pulse regularity and can generate irregular rhythm notifications, with a user-initiated single-lead electrocardiogram (ECG) that the device algorithm classifies as AF, sinus rhythm, or inconclusive [REF]. Large pragmatic studies and validation cohorts have reported high sensitivity and specificity for these algorithms [REF]. However, older adults were underrepresented in these studies, which enrolled predominantly younger and technologically engaged participants [REF]. Several studies also relied on short or intermittent reference recordings, or confirmed rhythm only in participants who received a positive notification, which can introduce partial verification bias and limits the estimation of sensitivity [REF].

Accuracy estimates obtained in younger populations may not apply to adults aged 75 years and older. Tremor, peripheral arterial disease, reduced peripheral perfusion, and frequent atrial or ventricular ectopy are common in this age group and can degrade PPG signal quality or produce false-positive and unclassifiable results [REF]. Recording a single-lead ECG requires the user to maintain finger contact with the device for approximately 30 seconds, which may be difficult for individuals with tremor, arthritis, or cognitive impairment [REF]. In addition, because predictive values depend on prevalence, the higher prevalence of AF in older adults alters the clinical meaning of both positive and negative results. Population-specific accuracy data are therefore needed to determine whether, and how, smartwatch-based AF detection should be used in this group.

We therefore conducted a prospective diagnostic accuracy study of [DEVICE NAME, MODEL, ALGORITHM/SOFTWARE VERSION] for the detection of AF in adults aged 75 years and older, using 14-day continuous ECG patch monitoring as the reference standard. The primary objective was to estimate the sensitivity and specificity of [the PPG-based irregular rhythm notification and/or the single-lead ECG classification] for AF at the participant level. Secondary objectives were to estimate positive and negative predictive values, the proportion of inconclusive recordings, and accuracy in prespecified subgroups [e.g., age ≥85 years, tremor, frequent ectopy].
