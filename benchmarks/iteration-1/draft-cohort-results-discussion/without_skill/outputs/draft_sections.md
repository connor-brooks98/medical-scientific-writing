## Results

### Primary Outcome

Within 90 days of discharge, CDI occurred in [n] of 2377 PPI users (4.1%) and [n] of 3837 nonusers (2.6%), an unadjusted absolute difference of 1.5 percentage points (95% CI, 0.6-2.4). After adjustment for age, sex, Charlson Comorbidity Index score, hospitalization in the prior 12 months, and antibiotic class received during the index stay, home PPI use was associated with a higher hazard of CDI (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR], 1.48; 95% CI, 1.12-1.96) (Table 2 and Figure [X]). In prespecified subgroup analyses by sex, the aHR was 1.81 (95% CI, 1.24-2.64) among men ([n] PPI users; [n] nonusers) and 1.21 (95% CI, 0.81-1.80) among women ([n] PPI users; [n] nonusers). The difference between these estimates was not statistically significant (P for interaction = .14) (Figure [Y]).

### Secondary Outcomes

All-cause mortality within 90 days was 11.8% ([n] of 2377) among PPI users and 11.2% ([n] of 3837) among nonusers (aHR, 1.03; 95% CI, 0.88-1.21). Readmission within 30 days occurred in 17.9% ([n] of 2377) and 16.4% ([n] of 3837), respectively (aHR, 1.06; 95% CI, 0.94-1.20). Neither secondary outcome differed significantly between groups (Table 2).

## Discussion

In this cohort study of 6214 adults aged 65 years or older hospitalized with community-acquired pneumonia, home PPI use documented at admission was associated with a 48% higher hazard of CDI in the 90 days after discharge. This association held after adjustment for demographic characteristics, comorbidity burden, recent hospitalization, and the class of antibiotic received during the index admission. In absolute terms, CDI developed in approximately 1 in 24 PPI users compared with 1 in 38 nonusers. PPI use was not significantly associated with 90-day mortality or 30-day readmission. However, the CIs for these outcomes include both no association and modest increases in risk (up to 21% for mortality and 20% for readmission), so these results do not show that PPI use is unrelated to either outcome. The point estimate for CDI was larger among men than among women, but the test for interaction was not significant. We therefore consider this difference exploratory, and our data do not support sex-specific conclusions.

These findings agree with a large body of observational evidence linking acid suppression to CDI. In a meta-analysis of 42 observational studies, Kwok et al^1^ reported a pooled odds ratio of 1.74 (95% CI, 1.47-2.85) for incident CDI among PPI users compared with nonusers, although heterogeneity across studies was substantial. They also found that concomitant use of PPIs and antibiotics conferred greater risk than PPI use alone. Our estimate points in the same direction and is somewhat smaller. Possible reasons include that we report a hazard ratio rather than an odds ratio, that we adjusted for inpatient antibiotic class, and that we restricted the outcome to the post-discharge period. The antibiotic interaction described by Kwok et al^1^ is especially relevant to our population: nearly all patients hospitalized with pneumonia receive antibiotics, and advanced age is itself a risk factor for CDI. PPI use has also been associated with recurrent CDI. Kwok et al^1^ reported a pooled odds ratio of 2.51 (95% CI, 1.16-5.44) across 3 studies, and McDonald et al^2^ found that continuous PPI use was associated with a higher hazard of recurrence (HR, 1.5; 95% CI, 1.1-2.0). The evidence is not uniform. Among 894 inpatients with incident CDI, Freedberg et al^3^ found no association between PPI receipt during CDI treatment and recurrence (HR, 0.82; 95% CI, 0.58-1.16). In a randomized trial of more than 17 000 participants, pantoprazole was not significantly associated with CDI, although CDI events were too few for a precise estimate.^4^ We did not evaluate recurrence. Our findings do suggest that discharge after pneumonia may be a reasonable time to reassess whether continued PPI therapy is indicated in older adults, a hypothesis that would need prospective evaluation.

### Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the observational design leaves room for residual confounding. PPI users may differ from nonusers in frailty, functional status, nursing facility residence, enteral feeding, prior CDI, and use of other acid suppressants, none of which the Charlson index fully captures. [Add if you ran it: The E-value for the primary estimate was 2.32 (1.49 for the lower confidence limit).] Second, PPI exposure was ascertained only from the admission medication reconciliation. We lacked information on dose, duration, adherence, over-the-counter use, and whether PPIs were continued, stopped, or started during hospitalization or at discharge. This misclassification is probably nondifferential with respect to outcome and would most likely bias estimates toward the null. Third, CDI was ascertained only from laboratory testing within our health system, so infections diagnosed at outside facilities were missed. If PPI users were more likely to return to our system or to undergo stool testing, detection bias could have inflated the association. The study period also spanned changes in diagnostic testing practices [specify, e.g., nucleic acid amplification testing vs toxin-based algorithms], which may have affected the distinction between colonization and infection. Fourth, we did not account for antibiotic exposure after discharge, a major CDI risk factor. Fifth, approximately 11% of patients died within 90 days, and death precludes CDI. Our Cox models estimate cause-specific hazards and should not be read as absolute risk under competing mortality [add if run: results were similar in a Fine-Gray subdistribution hazard model]. Sixth, we excluded 312 patients without a medication reconciliation. If missingness was related to illness severity or the admission pathway, this could have introduced selection bias. Seventh, the sex-specific subgroup analyses had limited power and were not adjusted for multiple comparisons. Finally, this was a single academic health system, and the study period included the COVID-19 pandemic, which altered pneumonia case mix, infection-control practices, and health care use. The findings may not generalize to other settings.
