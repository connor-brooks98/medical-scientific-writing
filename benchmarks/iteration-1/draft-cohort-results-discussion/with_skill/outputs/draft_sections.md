## Results

Of the 6214 patients included in the analysis, 2377 (38.3%) were taking a PPI at admission and 3837 were not. Within 90 days of discharge, CDI occurred in [n] of 2377 PPI users (4.1%) and [n] of 3837 nonusers (2.6%) (absolute difference, [DATA NEEDED: risk difference with 95% CI]). After adjustment for age, sex, Charlson Comorbidity Index score, hospitalization in the previous 12 months, and antibiotic class during the index stay, PPI use was associated with a higher hazard of CDI (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR], 1.48; 95% CI, 1.12-1.96). PPI use was not associated with 90-day all-cause mortality (11.8% vs 11.2%; aHR, 1.03; 95% CI, 0.88-1.21) or with 30-day readmission (17.9% vs 16.4%; aHR, 1.06; 95% CI, 0.94-1.20). In prespecified subgroup analyses, the aHR for CDI was 1.81 (95% CI, 1.24-2.64) in men and 1.21 (95% CI, 0.81-1.80) in women, and the test for interaction by sex was not statistically significant (P = .14) (Table [X]).

## Discussion

In this cohort of 6214 adults aged 65 years or older hospitalized with CAP at 1 academic health system from 2016 to 2023, home PPI use at admission was associated with a 48% higher adjusted hazard of CDI in the 90 days after discharge (4.1% vs 2.6%). PPI use was not associated with 90-day mortality or 30-day readmission. Neither null result excludes a small difference: the mortality estimate is compatible with a hazard 12% lower to 21% higher among PPI users, and the readmission estimate with a hazard 6% lower to 20% higher. The point estimate for CDI was larger in men (aHR, 1.81) than in women (aHR, 1.21). Because the interaction test does not support a difference by sex (P = .14) and the CI in women is wide, we do not interpret these estimates as evidence that the association differs between men and women.

Our estimate is somewhat lower than, and its CI includes, the pooled odds ratio (OR) for incident CDI with PPI use of 1.74 (95% CI, 1.47-2.85) reported by Kwok et al<sup>1</sup> from 39 observational studies, among which heterogeneity was substantial (I<sup>2</sup> = 85%). The same meta-analysis found a higher risk with concomitant PPI and antibiotic use than with PPI use alone (OR, 1.96; 95% CI, 1.03-3.70). All patients in our cohort received antibiotics for pneumonia during the index stay [CONFIRM], so our estimate describes PPI use in patients already exposed to antibiotics. A study restricted to critically ill patients did not find this association. Among 18 134 adults in 14 intensive care units (ICUs) at 3 hospitals, Faleck et al<sup>2</sup> found no association between PPIs and health care facility-onset CDI in patients who did not receive antibiotics (aHR, 1.56; 95% CI, 0.72-3.35) and a lower risk with PPIs in those who did (aHR, 0.64; 95% CI, 0.48-0.83). That study measured in-hospital PPI exposure and CDI during the ICU stay, whereas we measured home PPI use and CDI after discharge; the 2 studies therefore describe different periods of risk. We did not study recurrence, for which the evidence is mixed. Kwok et al<sup>1</sup> pooled 3 studies of recurrent CDI (OR, 2.51; 95% CI, 1.16-5.44), and McDonald et al<sup>3</sup> found that continuous PPI use was associated with recurrence within 90 days among 754 patients with health care-associated CDI (cause-specific HR, 1.5; 95% CI, 1.1-2.0). Freedberg et al,<sup>4</sup> however, found no association between PPI receipt during CDI treatment and recurrence among 894 inpatients (HR, 0.82; 95% CI, 0.58-1.16). ### Limitations

PPI exposure was recorded once, at admission, from medication reconciliation. We had no information on dose, duration, or adherence, or on whether the PPI was continued, stopped, or started at discharge; misclassification of this kind, if unrelated to CDI risk, would bias the HR toward the null. We identified CDI only from inpatient and outpatient laboratory results within our health system, so infections diagnosed elsewhere were missed. This would lower the observed absolute risk in both groups, and if PPI users were more likely than nonusers to receive follow-up care and testing within our system, it would also bias the HR away from the null. The model did not include antibiotic duration, frailty, or residence in a long-term care facility [CONFIRM: whether any of these were available]. If these factors were more common among PPI users, residual confounding would also bias the estimate away from the null. We excluded 312 patients (5.0% [CHECK DENOMINATOR]) without a documented medication reconciliation; the direction of any resulting bias depends on how these patients differed from those included [DATA NEEDED: comparison of excluded and included patients]. Finally, patients came from 1 academic health system, and absolute CDI risks may differ in community hospitals or in regions with different CDI incidence.
