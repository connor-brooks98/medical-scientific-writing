## Results

Of **[6,526]** patients aged 65 years or older who were hospitalized with community-acquired pneumonia between 2016 and 2023, 312 (**[4.8%]**) were excluded because admission medication reconciliation was missing. That left 6,214 patients in the analytic cohort (median [IQR] age, **[X (X-X)]** years; **[X (X%)]** women). At admission, 2,377 patients (38.3%) were documented as taking a proton pump inhibitor (PPI) at home and 3,837 (61.7%) were not (Table 1). Within 90 days of discharge, *Clostridioides difficile* infection (CDI) occurred in **[n]** PPI users (4.1%) and **[n]** nonusers (2.6%), an unadjusted absolute difference of 1.5 percentage points (95% CI, **[X to X]**). After adjustment for age, sex, Charlson Comorbidity Index score, hospitalization in the prior 12 months, and antibiotic class during the index admission, PPI use was associated with a higher hazard of CDI (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR], 1.48; 95% CI, 1.12-1.96) (Table 2). PPI use was not significantly associated with 90-day all-cause mortality (**[n]** [11.8%] vs **[n]** [11.2%]; aHR, 1.03; 95% CI, 0.88-1.21) or with 30-day readmission (**[n]** [17.9%] vs **[n]** [16.4%]; aHR, 1.06; 95% CI, 0.94-1.20). ## Discussion

In this cohort study of 6,214 adults aged 65 years or older hospitalized with community-acquired pneumonia at a single academic health system, home PPI use documented at admission was associated with a 48% higher hazard of CDI in the 90 days after discharge. This estimate was adjusted for demographic characteristics, comorbidity burden, recent hospitalization, and the class of antibiotic received during the index admission. The absolute difference was modest, 1.5 percentage points before adjustment. Still, PPI use was common in this population, with more than one-third of patients taking a PPI at admission, so even a modest excess risk could correspond to a meaningful number of CDI cases among older adults recovering from pneumonia. PPI use was not significantly associated with 90-day mortality or 30-day readmission. The upper confidence limits for these outcomes were consistent with relative increases of no more than about 20%. The association with CDI appeared stronger among men than women. However, the test for interaction was not significant, and we do not interpret this finding as evidence that the association differs by sex.

These findings are consistent with a meta-analysis of 42 observational studies (approximately 313,000 participants), which reported a pooled odds ratio of 1.74 for incident CDI among PPI users compared with nonusers [Kwok 2012]. That meta-analysis also found that PPI use combined with antibiotics was associated with greater risk than PPI use alone, with evidence of a synergistic interaction [Kwok 2012]. This is relevant to our cohort because nearly all patients hospitalized with pneumonia receive antibiotics **[confirm proportion]**. Our estimates therefore describe the risk associated with PPI use in a population with near-universal antibiotic exposure. This is the clinical setting in which the combined risk may matter most, and the decision to continue a PPI is one that can be changed. The magnitude of our estimate was somewhat smaller than the pooled estimate. This difference may reflect our cohort design and adjustment for antibiotic class. It may also reflect misclassification of exposure, which would be expected to bias the estimate toward the null (see Limitations). Evidence on recurrent CDI is less consistent. The same meta-analysis reported an association between PPI use and recurrence in a pooled analysis of 3 studies [Kwok 2012], whereas a cohort study of 894 inpatients with incident CDI found no association between concurrent PPI receipt and recurrence [Freedberg 2013]. **[Optional mechanistic sentence, if desired: PPI use has been shown to alter gastrointestinal microbial taxa associated with CDI [Freedberg 2015, *Gastroenterology*]; confirm before including.]** Taken together with prior work, our results suggest that hospitalization for pneumonia may be an appropriate point to reassess whether ongoing PPI therapy is indicated in older adults **[ref needed: PPI deprescribing guidance]**. Whether discontinuing PPIs at this point reduces CDI risk would need to be tested prospectively.

### Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, because of its observational design, residual confounding is possible. PPI users may have differed from nonusers in ways not captured by our adjustment set, such as frailty, long-term care residence, enteral feeding, outpatient antibiotic exposure before admission, or use of other acid-suppressive agents **[revise to match any additional covariates or sensitivity analyses]**. We also did not compare PPI users with an active comparator group, such as histamine-2 receptor antagonist users. Second, exposure was ascertained once, from medication reconciliation at admission. We had no information on PPI dose, duration, or adherence, and over-the-counter use may have been incompletely recorded. Some nonusers may have started a PPI during hospitalization or after discharge, and some users may have stopped. This misclassification would most likely bias estimates toward the null, but its direction cannot be guaranteed. Third, CDI was ascertained only from inpatient and outpatient laboratory testing within our health system, so cases diagnosed elsewhere were missed. If PPI users were more or less likely than nonusers to receive follow-up care or diarrhea testing within the system, this under-ascertainment could have been differential. In addition, **[specify assay, e.g., nucleic acid amplification testing]** may identify colonization rather than active infection in some patients. Fourth, approximately 11% of patients died within 90 days, and death precludes subsequent CDI. Our Cox models treated death as a censoring event and therefore estimate cause-specific hazards **[if performed: results were similar in a Fine-Gray competing risk analysis (eTable X)]**. Fifth, 312 patients were excluded because medication reconciliation was missing. If the missing reconciliation was related to illness severity or to the mode of admission, this exclusion could have introduced selection bias **[if available: excluded patients were similar to included patients in age, sex, and Charlson score (eTable X)]**. Sixth, the study was conducted in a single academic health system, which may limit generalizability to other settings and populations. Finally, the sex-stratified analyses were prespecified but had limited statistical power, and the absence of a significant interaction should not be taken as evidence that the associations in men and women are the same.
