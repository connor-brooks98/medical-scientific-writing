# Draft sections: home PPI use and post-discharge CDI after pneumonia admission

## Results

Of the 6,214 patients included, 2,377 were using a proton pump inhibitor (PPI) at admission and 3,837 were not; 312 patients (5.0%) were excluded because medication reconciliation was missing. *Clostridioides difficile* infection (CDI) within 90 days of discharge occurred in [DATA NEEDED: number of events] of 2,377 PPI users (4.1%) and [DATA NEEDED: number of events] of 3,837 nonusers (2.6%) (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR], 1.48; 95% CI, 1.12-1.96), after adjustment for age, sex, Charlson Comorbidity Index, hospitalization in the prior 12 months, and antibiotic class during the index stay. Ninety-day all-cause mortality was 11.8% among PPI users and 11.2% among nonusers (aHR, 1.03; 95% CI, 0.88-1.21). Thirty-day readmission was 17.9% and 16.4%, respectively (aHR, 1.06; 95% CI, 0.94-1.20). In prespecified subgroup analyses by sex, the aHR for CDI was 1.81 (95% CI, 1.24-2.64) in men and 1.21 (95% CI, 0.81-1.80) in women (*P* = .14 for interaction).

## Discussion

In this cohort of 6,214 adults aged 65 years or older hospitalized with community-acquired pneumonia at a single academic health system, home PPI use at admission was associated with a higher rate of CDI in the 90 days after discharge (4.1% vs 2.6%; aHR, 1.48; 95% CI, 1.12-1.96), an unadjusted absolute difference of 1.5 percentage points. PPI use was not associated with 90-day mortality or 30-day readmission. Both estimates were close to 1, but the upper confidence limits (1.21 for mortality and 1.20 for readmission) mean these data do not exclude moderate increases in either outcome. The CDI estimate was higher in men than in women, but the interaction test did not show that the association differed by sex (*P* = .14), and the subgroup confidence intervals overlapped (1.24-2.64 in men; 0.81-1.80 in women). We therefore take the overall estimate as the main result and do not interpret the sex-specific estimates as evidence of effect modification.

In a meta-analysis by Kwok et al of 42 observational studies (30 case-control and 12 cohort; 313,000 participants), PPI use was associated with incident CDI in a pooled analysis of 39 studies (odds ratio [OR], 1.74; 95% CI, 1.47-2.85), with substantial heterogeneity (*I*<sup>2</sup> = 85%).[1] Our estimate is somewhat smaller, near the lower limit of that CI; because CDI occurred in fewer than 5% of each group, the OR approximates the HR. In the same meta-analysis, concurrent PPI and antibiotic use was associated with greater risk than PPI use alone (OR, 1.96; 95% CI, 1.03-3.70).[1] Because patients admitted with pneumonia receive antibiotics [DATA NEEDED: proportion of the cohort receiving antibiotics during the index stay], our estimate describes PPI use after recent antibiotic exposure, the combination associated with greater risk in that analysis. Evidence on recurrent CDI, which we did not study, is less consistent: Kwok et al pooled 3 studies (OR, 2.51; 95% CI, 1.16-5.44),[1] whereas Freedberg et al found no association between in-hospital PPI receipt during CDI treatment and recurrence among 894 inpatients (HR, 0.82; 95% CI, 0.58-1.16).[2] Most studies in the meta-analysis used a case-control design; ours is a cohort restricted to older adults after a pneumonia admission, with CDI counted from inpatient and outpatient testing for 90 days after discharge.

### Limitations

This study has limitations. First, PPI users may differ from nonusers in frailty, gastrointestinal disease, polypharmacy, and contact with health care in ways that the Charlson index and prior hospitalization do not capture [DATA NEEDED: whether nursing facility residence, prior CDI, and histamine-2 receptor antagonist use were measured and adjusted for]. Residual confounding of this kind would most likely bias the HR away from the null. Second, PPI use was recorded only at admission, without dose, duration, adherence, or changes at discharge. The resulting misclassification is probably nondifferential and would bias the HR toward the null; it also precluded any analysis of dose or duration. Third, CDI was ascertained only from laboratory tests within our health system. Diagnoses made elsewhere were missed, which lowers absolute risks in both groups but biases the HR only if capture differed by PPI status. If PPI users, who may have had more prior hospitalizations, were more likely to return to our system or to be tested for diarrhea, the HR would be biased away from the null [DATA NEEDED: CDI assay (toxin immunoassay, PCR, or 2-step algorithm), because PCR alone can detect colonization]. Fourth, excluding the 312 patients without medication reconciliation could bias the estimate in either direction if missingness was related to both PPI use and CDI risk [DATA NEEDED: characteristics of excluded vs included patients]. Fifth, death precludes CDI, but 90-day mortality was similar in the 2 groups (11.8% vs 11.2%), so a differential competing risk is unlikely to explain the CDI difference [DATA NEEDED: how death was handled in the Cox model, eg, censoring vs Fine-Gray]. Sixth, the secondary outcomes and sex subgroups were not adjusted for multiple comparisons [confirm]. Finally, all patients were treated at a single academic health system between 2016 and 2023, and the results may not apply to community hospitals.
