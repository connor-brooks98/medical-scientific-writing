Introduction

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is the most common sustained cardiac arrhythmia, and its prevalence rises steeply with age [R1]. AF increases the risk of ischemic stroke approximately fivefold [R2], and because it is frequently asymptomatic, stroke may be its first clinical manifestation [R3]. These observations have prompted interest in screening older adults, although recommendations differ: European guidelines support opportunistic screening in older adults and state that systematic screening may be considered from age 75 years [R4], whereas the US Preventive Services Task Force concluded that the evidence is insufficient to assess the balance of benefits and harms of screening [R5]. Randomized trials of population screening have shown modest or nonsignificant reductions in stroke [R6], and the benefit of anticoagulation for short episodes of device-detected AF remains uncertain [R7].

Consumer smartwatches can identify possible AF through photoplethysmography (PPG)-based irregular rhythm algorithms and on-demand single-lead electrocardiography (ECG) [R8]. In large prospective studies, smartwatch irregular rhythm notifications have shown high positive predictive values for AF [R9], and automated single-lead ECG classification has shown high sensitivity and specificity in validation cohorts [R10]. However, these studies enrolled predominantly younger participants [R9,R10], and their estimates may not apply to adults aged 75 years and older, who have the highest AF prevalence and are the group in whom screening is most often considered. Several age-related factors could reduce device performance in this population. Tremor and peripheral vascular disease may degrade PPG and ECG signal quality, frequent atrial and ventricular ectopy may mimic the irregularity that PPG algorithms detect, and reduced dexterity may hinder correct ECG recording [R11]. These factors could increase false-positive results and the proportion of recordings that cannot be classified. In older adults, false-positive results carry particular costs, including further cardiac testing, patient anxiety, and potential exposure to anticoagulation in a population at elevated bleeding risk [R12].

We therefore conducted a prospective study to evaluate the diagnostic accuracy of a commercially available smartwatch for detecting AF in adults aged 75 years and older, using 14-day continuous ECG patch monitoring as the reference standard. Our primary objective was to estimate the sensitivity and specificity of the smartwatch for AF in this population.
