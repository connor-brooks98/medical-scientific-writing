## Results

### Primary and Secondary Outcomes

Of the 6,214 patients in the analytic cohort, 2,377 (38.3%) were taking a PPI at admission. *Clostridioides difficile* infection (CDI) within 90 days of discharge occurred in [97] of 2,377 PPI users (4.1%) and [100] of 3,837 nonusers (2.6%), an unadjusted absolute difference of 1.5 percentage points. After adjustment for age, sex, Charlson Comorbidity Index score, hospitalization in the previous 12 months, and antibiotic class received during the index admission, PPI use was associated with a higher hazard of CDI (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR], 1.48; 95% CI, 1.12-1.96). In prespecified subgroup analyses, the aHR was 1.81 (95% CI, 1.24-2.64) among men and 1.21 (95% CI, 0.81-1.80) among women; the interaction between PPI use and sex was not statistically significant (*P* = .14). For the secondary outcomes, 90-day all-cause mortality was 11.8% ([280] of 2,377) among PPI users and 11.2% ([430] of 3,837) among nonusers (aHR, 1.03; 95% CI, 0.88-1.21), and 30-day readmission was 17.9% ([425] of 2,377) and 16.4% ([629] of 3,837), respectively (aHR, 1.06; 95% CI, 0.94-1.20).

## Discussion

In this retrospective cohort study of 6,214 adults aged 65 years or older hospitalized with community-acquired pneumonia at a single academic health system, PPI use at admission was associated with a 48% higher hazard of CDI in the 90 days after discharge. The estimate accounted for age, sex, comorbidity burden, recent hospitalization, and the class of antibiotic given during the index stay. In absolute terms, the difference was 1.5 percentage points (4.1% vs 2.6%), which is small for any one patient but applies to a large population: more than one-third of this cohort was taking a PPI at admission. We found no association between PPI use and 90-day mortality or 30-day readmission, although the confidence intervals for both outcomes are compatible with modest increases in risk (up to 21% for mortality and 20% for readmission). The point estimate for CDI was larger in men than in women, but the test for interaction was not significant, and the subgroup estimates should not be read as evidence that the association differs by sex.

Our findings are consistent with earlier observational work. A meta-analysis of 42 observational studies (more than 300,000 participants) reported a pooled odds ratio for incident CDI of 1.74 (95% CI, 1.47-2.85) among PPI users compared with nonusers, with substantial heterogeneity across studies (*I*^2^ = 85%).^1^ The same analysis found that PPI use combined with antibiotics carried a higher risk than PPI use alone (odds ratio, 1.96; 95% CI, 1.03-3.70).^1^ This point is relevant to our cohort, in which nearly all patients received antibiotics for pneumonia [confirm the proportion]; restricting the study to a uniformly antibiotic-exposed population and adjusting for antibiotic class reduces, though does not remove, confounding by antibiotic exposure, which has been a central concern in this literature. Evidence on recurrent CDI is less consistent. The meta-analysis pooled 3 studies and reported an odds ratio of 2.51 (95% CI, 1.16-5.44),^1^ whereas a cohort of 894 inpatients with incident CDI found no association between PPIs given during CDI treatment and recurrence (HR, 0.82; 95% CI, 0.58-1.16).^2^ We did not examine recurrence. A proposed mechanism is alteration of the gut microbiome: in a small crossover trial in healthy volunteers, 4 to 8 weeks of high-dose omeprazole increased taxa associated with CDI and decreased Clostridiales.^3^ [Ref 3 is a suggested addition; delete this sentence if you do not want a mechanistic citation here.]

### Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, as in any observational study, residual confounding is possible. PPI users may differ from nonusers in frailty, functional status, residence in a long-term care facility, prior CDI, and gastrointestinal comorbidity, none of which were included in our model [revise if any were captured]. Second, PPI exposure was defined from the admission medication reconciliation alone. We did not know dose, duration, or adherence, whether PPIs were started or stopped during the hospitalization, or whether patients were taking a PPI at discharge; over-the-counter use may also have been missed. Patients classified as nonusers who received a PPI in the hospital or after discharge would bias the estimate toward the null if this misclassification was unrelated to CDI risk. Third, CDI was ascertained only from laboratory testing within our health system, so infections diagnosed elsewhere were missed. If PPI users were more or less likely than nonusers to return to our system for care or to be tested for C difficile, this ascertainment could be differential. [State the testing method used during 2016-2023, eg, nucleic acid amplification test alone vs a multistep algorithm; nucleic acid amplification testing alone can detect colonization as well as infection.] Fourth, death occurred in about 11% of patients within 90 days, and a Cox model that censors at death can overestimate cumulative CDI incidence [if you ran a Fine-Gray or other competing-risk sensitivity analysis, report it here; if not, consider running one]. Fifth, 312 patients (4.8% of those otherwise eligible) were excluded because medication reconciliation was missing, and the results may not apply to them if they differed systematically from included patients. Sixth, the subgroup analyses had limited power to detect effect modification. Finally, the data come from a single academic health system, and the findings may not generalize to other settings or to patients younger than 65 years.

## Notes for the authors (delete before submission)

1. **Citation correction (Freedberg).** The Freedberg 2015 *Clin Infect Dis* paper (doi:10.1093/cid/civ432) is a nested case-control study of acid suppression and CDI in infants and children. It is not about recurrent CDI. Freedberg's recurrence paper is the 2013 *Am J Gastroenterol* cohort (ref 2 above), and it found **no** association between PPIs and recurrence (HR, 0.82; 95% CI, 0.58-1.16). I cited it for what it actually reports. The recurrent-CDI association you had in mind is in the Kwok meta-analysis (OR, 2.51, from 3 studies).
2. **Kwok numbers.** Pooled OR 1.74 (95% CI, 1.47-2.85; I^2^ = 85%; 39 studies pooled for incident CDI). The CI is not symmetric around 1.74 on the log scale; that is how the abstract reports it. Check it against the full text before you quote it.
3. **Exclusion percentage.** 312/6,214 = 5.0%, but 312 of the 6,526 patients otherwise eligible (6,214 + 312) is 4.8%. The second is the usual denominator; I used 4.8%. Update the flow diagram and Methods so they match.

Correct anything that does not match your Methods.
