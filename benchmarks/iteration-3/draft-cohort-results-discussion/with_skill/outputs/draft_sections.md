## Results

Of the 6,214 patients in the analytic cohort, 2,377 were taking a PPI at admission and 3,837 were not. Within 90 days of discharge, CDI occurred in [DATA NEEDED: No.] PPI users (4.1%) and [DATA NEEDED: No.] nonusers (2.6%). After adjustment for age, sex, Charlson Comorbidity Index, hospitalization in the prior 12 months, and antibiotic class during the index stay, PPI use was associated with a higher hazard of CDI (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR], 1.48; 95% CI, 1.12-1.96). PPI use was not significantly associated with either secondary outcome. All-cause mortality at 90 days was 11.8% among PPI users and 11.2% among nonusers (aHR, 1.03; 95% CI, 0.88-1.21), and 30-day readmission was 17.9% and 16.4%, respectively (aHR, 1.06; 95% CI, 0.94-1.20). In the prespecified analysis by sex, the aHR for CDI was 1.81 (95% CI, 1.24-2.64) in men and 1.21 (95% CI, 0.81-1.80) in women; the difference between men and women was not statistically significant (P for interaction = .14).

## Discussion

In this cohort of 6,214 adults aged 65 years or older hospitalized with CAP, home PPI use at admission was associated with a higher rate of CDI in the 90 days after discharge (4.1% vs 2.6%; aHR, 1.48; 95% CI, 1.12-1.96), an absolute difference of 1.5 percentage points [DATA NEEDED: 95% CI for the risk difference]. PPI use was not significantly associated with 90-day mortality or 30-day readmission. For both outcomes, though, the upper confidence limits (1.21 and 1.20) are compatible with hazards about 20% higher in PPI users. The point estimate for CDI was larger in men (aHR, 1.81) than in women (aHR, 1.21), but the interaction test did not show a difference by sex (P = .14), and we do not interpret the sex-specific estimates as evidence of effect modification.

Our estimate is in the same direction as the meta-analysis by Kwok et al,[1] which pooled 39 observational studies and found higher odds of incident CDI among PPI users than nonusers (OR, 1.74; 95% CI, 1.47-2.85), with substantial heterogeneity across studies (I² = 85%). The hazard ratio in our cohort is smaller than that pooled odds ratio. The intervals overlap, however, and odds ratios pooled mostly from case-control studies are not directly comparable with hazard ratios from a cohort followed for a fixed period. Kwok et al also found higher odds of CDI with concurrent PPI and antibiotic use than with PPI use alone (OR, 1.96; 95% CI, 1.03-3.70).[1] Patients in our cohort were admitted for pneumonia, and [DATA NEEDED: No. (%)] received antibiotics during the index stay, so our estimate describes PPI use in patients with recent antibiotic exposure, the combination for which Kwok et al found the greater risk. [CITATION NEEDED: prior cohort studies of PPI use and CDI restricted to older adults or to patients hospitalized with pneumonia, to compare this population with the mixed populations in the meta-analysis.] For recurrent CDI, which we did not study, the evidence is less consistent: Kwok et al pooled 3 studies (OR, 2.51; 95% CI, 1.16-5.44),[1] whereas Freedberg et al found no association between PPI receipt during CDI treatment and recurrence among 894 inpatients with incident CDI (HR, 0.82; 95% CI, 0.58-1.16).[2]

### Limitations

PPI exposure was recorded once, from the admission medication reconciliation, and we had no data on dose, duration, adherence, or changes at or after discharge. Patients who stopped a PPI after discharge counted as exposed, and nonusers who started one, or whose over-the-counter use was missing from the medication list, counted as unexposed. If this misclassification is unrelated to CDI risk, it biases the hazard ratio toward the null. CDI was ascertained only from laboratory tests performed within our health system, so infections diagnosed elsewhere were missed. This undercounts absolute risk in both groups; it biases the hazard ratio only if PPI users and nonusers differed in where they received follow-up care or how often they were tested for diarrhea, and more frequent testing of PPI users would bias it away from the null. [DATA NEEDED: CDI assay (toxin, PCR, or 2-step algorithm) and whether it changed during 2016-2023.] Because PPI use was not randomized, residual confounding is likely. The models did not include frailty, long-term care residence, prior CDI, or antibiotic use after discharge [DATA NEEDED: confirm which of these were unmeasured or not modeled]; if PPI users carried more of these risks, the estimate would be biased away from the null. We excluded 312 patients (5.0%) without a medication reconciliation, which could bias the estimate in either direction if missing reconciliation was related to illness severity. Finally, the 2.6% CDI rate among nonusers and the antibiotic choices for CAP reflect the case mix and prescribing practices of one academic health system.
