**What was off**

Most of the problem is staging. The draft sets up claims and objections that nobody made, then knocks them down, and some of those moves claim more than the data show.

1. **Strawman objections.** "This is not to say that delayed mobilization is always harmful" rejects a claim no one made. "One might argue that patients with severe dementia cannot participate... but our findings suggest otherwise" invents an objection and then answers it with the delirium result, which has nothing to do with whether patients with severe dementia can take part in physiotherapy. No severity-stratified data are reported, so I removed the rebuttal and stated that limitation directly.
2. **"Not X, it means Y" framing.** The two sentences explaining what a null delirium result does and doesn't mean make a valid point, but in a lecturing tone. I cut them to one plain sentence about power and scope.
3. **Throat-clearing and aphorism.** "At its core, the central question..." and "Mobility is the currency of recovery" add nothing, so I cut them. The opening now states the main finding.
4. **The question doesn't match the outcomes.** The draft frames the study as asking whether early mobilization is *safe*, but its headline results are about benefit (mortality and length of stay). The revision reports what was found without forcing that framing.
5. **Causal overreach.** "This illustrates the importance of early mobilization" and "early mobilization appears to be beneficial" are causal claims from an observational association. The revision says "associated with" throughout and ends with what a trial would need to show.
6. **Treating missing data as evidence.** Saying the 18% with missing ambulatory status were "likely nonambulatory" is a guess presented as a finding. It also points to the study's biggest weakness without naming it: confounding by indication. Patients who walked before the fracture are more likely to be mobilized early *and* to survive. The revision states this directly and says which way the bias would push the result.
7. **A feasibility claim with no number.** "Early mobilization was feasible for most patients" isn't backed by any figure in the section.
8. **A reference to a table that isn't there.** "The table below" points to nothing, and Discussion sections usually don't carry tables anyway.

**Assumptions and placeholders**

- I read "31% vs 29%" as early vs delayed, the same order as the other comparisons. If it's the other way round, change "early and delayed" in that sentence.
- Bracketed placeholders mark things only you can fill in: how many patients were mobilized within 24 hours (needed to support the feasibility claim), the comparison with prior cohorts and their citations, the adjustment covariates, any sensitivity analysis for the missing ambulatory data, results by dementia severity, single- vs multicenter design, and any other limitations. I didn't invent any numbers or references.
- I added a short limitations paragraph and a conclusion, because the draft had neither.
