**What changed**

- **Structure:** The intro now runs known, then unknown, then your study, in three paragraphs. The "greatest burden in older adults" point moved into the first sentence, so the opening leads straight to your population instead of a general burden statement ("major global health burden, affecting millions", "growing interest in wearables").
- **Unsupported claims:** "Studies have shown" and "a growing body of evidence" had no named studies behind them. They are now specific claims with placeholders for the citation and the sensitivity and specificity figures. I kept your word "high" until the numbers are in.
- **The gap:** "Remains poorly understood" is now a population gap you can back with data (how many people aged 75 or older the earlier validation studies enrolled). If that number is small, it is the strongest sentence in the intro.
- **Last paragraph:** I cut "To address this critical gap", "comprehensive", and the whole closing sentence ("not only fills an important gap... pave the way for future research"). An Introduction ends on the question and design, and editors cut that kind of self-promotion anyway.
- **Sentences and words:** I removed the ", highlighting the need for..." tail and the stacked hedge "promising tools that could potentially transform the landscape". I also removed "Furthermore" and "Moreover", which opened consecutive sentences, and the em-dash pair. "Comorbidities such as tremor" became a plain list, because tremor is a sign rather than a comorbidity.
- **Length:** 124 words not counting placeholders, down from 220. The length you get back depends on the citations and numbers you add.

**Fidelity:** All facts from the draft are still there: the PPG and single-lead ECG modalities, the comorbidities, the prospective design, the age 75 and older population, and the 14-day patch reference standard. No numbers or references were added. The checker found no numbers lost or introduced.

**Placeholders and author queries**

1. Seven placeholders are in the text (prevalence by age, the sensitivity and specificity ranges plus their sources, stroke outcome evidence, the age makeup of earlier validation cohorts, and signal-quality effects).
2. **Stroke sentence.** I changed "may lead to improved outcomes, including reduced stroke risk" to "may reduce stroke risk". Please word it only as strongly as your citation supports. The evidence that screen-detected AF benefits from treatment is mixed. Candidates to check, recalled from memory and **not verified** (confirm details before citing): STROKESTOP (Svennberg et al., Lancet 2021; population screening of 75- to 76-year-olds), LOOP (Svendsen et al., Lancet 2021; implantable loop recorder screening in 70- to 90-year-olds), and NOAH-AFNET 6 (Kirchhof et al., NEJM 2023) and ARTESiA (Healey et al., NEJM 2024) on anticoagulation for device-detected AF.
3. **Gap data.** Check the age distribution in the large smartwatch studies you plan to cite (for example the Apple Heart Study, Perez et al., NEJM 2019, and the Fitbit Heart Study, Lubitz et al., Circulation 2022; also unverified). If few participants were 75 or older, give that number in place of "is uncertain".
4. **Ectopy.** Frequent ectopy mainly creates irregular rhythms that algorithms can mistake for AF, which lowers specificity. It does not mainly degrade signal quality. Consider "may degrade signal quality or mimic AF" if your data or sources support that.
5. **Index test (STARD 2015).** Consider naming the device, the algorithm or software version, and the mode tested (PPG notification, on-demand ECG, or both) in the last sentence. You could also add your primary accuracy measures or hypothesis (STARD item 4), for example "We aimed to estimate sensitivity and specificity..."
6. If you want a global burden figure, add one with its source in the first sentence. I removed "affecting millions" rather than leave an unsourced number.
