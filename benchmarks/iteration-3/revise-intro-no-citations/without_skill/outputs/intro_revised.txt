Introduction

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is the most common sustained arrhythmia in adults, and its prevalence rises steeply with age [REF]. Adults aged 75 years and older account for a disproportionate share of both AF cases and AF-related ischemic strokes [REF]. Because AF is often paroxysmal or asymptomatic, many patients are not diagnosed until after a stroke or another complication has occurred [REF].

Consumer smartwatches offer a way to detect AF outside the clinic. Most use photoplethysmography (PPG) to identify irregular pulse intervals, and some models also record a user-initiated single-lead electrocardiogram (ECG). In validation studies, these algorithms have detected AF with high sensitivity and specificity [REF; add reported ranges]. Whether earlier detection improves outcomes is less certain: randomized trials of systematic AF screening have increased AF diagnosis and anticoagulant use, but their effects on stroke have been modest or not statistically significant [REF]. Any screening strategy, however, depends on a detection method whose accuracy is known in the population being screened.

That accuracy has not been established in adults aged 75 years and older, who have been underrepresented in smartwatch validation cohorts [REF]. Several conditions common in this age group could alter device performance. Tremor and motion artifact degrade the PPG signal, peripheral vascular disease and reduced skin perfusion weaken the pulse waveform, and frequent atrial or ventricular ectopy produces irregular pulse intervals that an algorithm may classify as AF [REF]. These factors could lower sensitivity, increase false-positive notifications, or raise the proportion of recordings that cannot be classified. Because sensitivity and specificity vary with the patient spectrum in which they are measured, estimates from younger cohorts cannot be assumed to apply to older adults [REF].
