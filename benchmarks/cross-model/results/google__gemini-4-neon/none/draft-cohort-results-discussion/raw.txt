# Draft Manuscript Sections (*JAMA Network Open*)

## Results

### Primary and Secondary Outcomes

Among the 6214 older adults hospitalized with community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) included in the cohort, 2377 (38.3%) had documented home proton pump inhibitor (PPI) use at admission and 3837 (61.7%) were nonusers. Within 90 days of hospital discharge, *Clostridioides difficile* infection (CDI) occurred in 4.1% of PPI users compared with 2.6% of nonusers. In the multivariable Cox proportional hazards model adjusting for age, sex, Charlson Comorbidity Index, prior hospitalization within the preceding 12 months, and antibiotic class administered during the index hospitalization, home PPI use at admission was associated with a higher hazard of 90-day CDI (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR], 1.48; 95% CI, 1.12-1.96). In contrast, no statistically significant differences were observed between PPI users and nonusers for either secondary outcome, including 90-day all-cause mortality (11.8% vs 11.2%; aHR, 1.03; 95% CI, 0.88-1.21) and 30-day hospital readmission (17.9% vs 16.4%; aHR, 1.06; 95% CI, 0.94-1.20). In prespecified subgroup analyses stratified by sex, the adjusted association between home PPI use and 90-day CDI was statistically significant among men (aHR, 1.81; 95% CI, 1.24-2.64) and smaller and not statistically significant among women (aHR, 1.21; 95% CI, 0.81-1.80); however, the test for interaction did not show statistically significant evidence of effect modification by sex (*P* = .14 for interaction).

## Discussion

In this retrospective cohort study of 6214 adults aged 65 years or older hospitalized with CAP at a single academic health system between 2016 and 2023, home PPI use documented at admission was associated with a 48% higher adjusted hazard of CDI within 90 days of discharge (4.1% vs 2.6%; aHR, 1.48; 95% CI, 1.12-1.96), independent of age, sex, comorbidity burden, recent hospitalization, and inpatient antibiotic class. By contrast, home PPI use was not associated with 90-day all-cause mortality or 30-day hospital readmission. Although subgroup point estimates for postdischarge CDI were numerically larger among men than among women, the absence of a statistically significant interaction (*P* = .14) indicates insufficient evidence of differential risk by sex.

These findings align with prior observational evidence linking acid-suppressive therapy to CDI and extend it to a high-risk population of older adults hospitalized for CAP. In a meta-analysis of observational studies, Kwok et al^1^ found that PPI use was associated with an increased risk of incident CDI (pooled odds ratio, approximately 1.74), and Freedberg et al^2^ similarly demonstrated an association between PPI exposure and recurrent CDI. Biologically, gastric acid suppression may impair acid-mediated host defense against *C difficile* and disrupt gastrointestinal microbial composition, potentially compounding the dysbiosis induced by systemic antibiotic therapy administered during pneumonia hospitalization [Ref]. Although home PPI use did not translate into higher short-term all-cause readmission or 90-day mortality in our cohort, postdischarge CDI remains a clinically important and potentially preventable cause of morbidity among older adults. Consequently, hospitalization for CAP—a setting in which antimicrobial exposure is nearly universal—may represent a practical clinical juncture to reassess the ongoing indication for chronic PPI therapy and consider deprescribing when acid suppression is no longer warranted [Ref].

### Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, because the cohort was drawn from a single academic health system, the findings may not be generalizable to community hospitals, other health systems, or different patient populations. Second, as with any retrospective observational study, residual and unmeasured confounding cannot be excluded despite multivariable adjustment; factors such as the clinical indication for PPI therapy, pneumonia severity, frailty, postdischarge antibiotic exposure, and postacute care setting were not captured in our models. Third, PPI exposure was ascertained solely from home medication reconciliation at admission, without data on dose, duration of prior use, adherence, over-the-counter use, inpatient continuation or new initiation, or postdischarge discontinuation, which may have resulted in exposure misclassification. Fourth, 312 patients (5.0%) were excluded because of missing admission medication reconciliation data, introducing potential selection bias if missingness was related to exposure or outcomes. Finally, CDI was identified exclusively through inpatient and outpatient laboratory testing within our health system; infections diagnosed or treated at outside facilities would not have been captured, potentially underestimating the true postdischarge incidence of CDI.
