## Results

### Primary and secondary outcomes

Among 6,214 adults aged 65 years or older admitted with community-acquired pneumonia between 2016 and 2023, 2,377 used a home proton pump inhibitor (PPI) at admission and 3,837 did not. Within 90 days of discharge, *Clostridioides difficile* infection (CDI) occurred in 4.1% of PPI users vs 2.6% of nonusers [DATA NEEDED: event counts (n) in each group]. Home PPI use was associated with a higher hazard of 90-day CDI in Cox proportional hazards regression (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR], 1.48; 95% CI, 1.12-1.96), after adjustment for age, sex, Charlson Comorbidity Index, prior hospitalization within 12 months, and antibiotic class during the index stay. Neither secondary outcome differed significantly between groups. Ninety-day all-cause mortality was 11.8% among PPI users vs 11.2% among nonusers (aHR, 1.03; 95% CI, 0.88-1.21), and 30-day readmission was 17.9% vs 16.4% (aHR, 1.06; 95% CI, 0.94-1.20) [DATA NEEDED: event counts (n) for both secondary outcomes]. In prespecified subgroup analyses by sex, the aHR for 90-day CDI was 1.81 (95% CI, 1.24-2.64) among men and 1.21 (95% CI, 0.81-1.80) among women, with no statistically significant interaction (*P* = .14) [DATA NEEDED: subgroup event counts and percentages (n/N, %) by sex].

## Discussion

In this retrospective cohort of 6,214 older adults hospitalized with community-acquired pneumonia, home PPI use at admission was associated with a higher hazard of CDI within 90 days of discharge (4.1% vs 2.6%; aHR, 1.48; 95% CI, 1.12-1.96). This association held after adjustment for age, sex, Charlson Comorbidity Index, prior hospitalization, and inpatient antibiotic class. By contrast, home PPI use was not associated with 90-day all-cause mortality (aHR, 1.03; 95% CI, 0.88-1.21) or 30-day readmission (aHR, 1.06; 95% CI, 0.94-1.20), outcomes shaped by many competing causes in older adults recovering from pneumonia. Although the point estimate for CDI was higher in men than in women (aHR, 1.81 vs 1.21), the interaction test was not statistically significant (*P* = .14), so these data do not support differential risk by sex.

Our primary finding aligns in direction with earlier observational studies, although the magnitude is smaller than pooled estimates from broader populations. In a 2012 meta-analysis, Kwok et al [CITATION NEEDED: full citation for Kwok et al, *Am J Gastroenterol*, 2012] reported an association between PPI use and CDI, with an odds ratio of approximately 1.74 [DATA NEEDED: exact pooled OR, 95% CI, and number of studies and participants]. Freedberg et al [CITATION NEEDED: full citation for Freedberg et al, *Clin Infect Dis*, 2015] likewise found that PPI use was associated with recurrent CDI [DATA NEEDED: study design, population, and effect estimate with 95% CI], whereas our cohort evaluated post-discharge CDI after hospitalization for pneumonia. The more modest aHR of 1.48 in our cohort may reflect population restriction and confounding adjustment. All patients were hospitalized with community-acquired pneumonia, and our models accounted for inpatient antibiotic class; comparing PPI users and nonusers within one acute inpatient indication can produce a smaller relative hazard than pooling heterogeneous cohorts that include community or lower-risk patients. Even smaller or null associations have been reported in studies using randomized designs or active comparators to limit confounding by indication [CITATION NEEDED: randomized or active-comparator studies of PPIs and CDI]. Biologically, gastric acid suppression may alter the gut microbiota or permit survival of vegetative *C. difficile* organisms after antibiotic exposure [CITATION NEEDED: mechanistic reference, if desired].

### Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, because this was a retrospective observational study, residual confounding by frailty, illness severity, or unmeasured factors related to the indication for acid suppression cannot be excluded. Such confounding would typically bias the hazard ratio upward. Second, PPI exposure was defined from home medication reconciliation at admission only, without data on prior duration, adherence, or whether PPIs were stopped or started after admission. Nondifferential exposure misclassification of this kind generally biases estimates toward the null. Third, CDI was ascertained solely from inpatient and outpatient laboratory testing within our health system, so infections diagnosed at outside facilities were missed. This incomplete capture underestimates absolute 90-day CDI incidence and could bias the hazard ratio if outside care differed between PPI users and nonusers. Fourth, excluding 312 patients (5.0%) who lacked medication reconciliation data may have introduced selection bias of uncertain direction. Finally, because all patients were treated within a single academic health system, these findings may not generalize to community hospitals or settings with different antibiotic prescribing and CDI testing practices.
