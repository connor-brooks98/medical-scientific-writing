## Results

Among the 6214 patients (2377 home proton pump inhibitor [PPI] users and 3837 nonusers), *Clostridioides difficile* infection (CDI) occurred within 90 days of discharge in [DATA NEEDED: n] PPI users (4.1%) vs [DATA NEEDED: n] nonusers (2.6%) ([DATA NEEDED: Table/Figure reference]). In Cox proportional hazards models adjusted for age, sex, Charlson Comorbidity Index, hospitalization in the prior 12 months, and antibiotic class during the index stay, home PPI use was associated with a higher hazard of 90-day CDI (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR], 1.48; 95% CI, 1.12–1.96). PPI use was not associated with 90-day all-cause mortality ([DATA NEEDED: n] [11.8%] vs [DATA NEEDED: n] [11.2%]; aHR, 1.03; 95% CI, 0.88–1.21) or 30-day readmission ([DATA NEEDED: n] [17.9%] vs [DATA NEEDED: n] [16.4%]; aHR, 1.06; 95% CI, 0.94–1.20). In prespecified subgroup analyses by sex, the aHR for 90-day CDI was 1.81 (95% CI, 1.24–2.64) among men and 1.21 (95% CI, 0.81–1.80) among women. The test for interaction by sex was not statistically significant (P = .14).

## Discussion

In this single-system cohort of 6214 adults aged 65 years or older hospitalized with community-acquired pneumonia from 2016 to 2023, home PPI use at admission was associated with a higher adjusted hazard of 90-day CDI (4.1% vs 2.6%; aHR, 1.48; 95% CI, 1.12–1.96). By contrast, PPI use was not associated with 90-day all-cause mortality (11.8% vs 11.2%; aHR, 1.03; 95% CI, 0.88–1.21) or 30-day readmission (17.9% vs 16.4%; aHR, 1.06; 95% CI, 0.94–1.20). In prespecified subgroup analyses, the aHR for CDI was 1.81 (95% CI, 1.24–2.64) in men and 1.21 (95% CI, 0.81–1.80) in women. However, the interaction test was not statistically significant (P = .14); these data do not establish a sex-specific difference. Because 90-day mortality (11.2%) and 30-day readmission (16.4%) were common even among nonusers, the smaller absolute difference in CDI incidence (4.1% vs 2.6%) would not be expected to produce a detectable difference in either aggregate outcome.

Our primary finding is consistent with prior observational studies reporting an association between PPI use and CDI. In a 2012 meta-analysis, Kwok et al found that PPI use was associated with CDI, with a pooled odds ratio of approximately 1.74 [DATA NEEDED: exact pooled OR, 95% CI, and number of studies and participants from Kwok et al]. The adjusted hazard ratio in our cohort (1.48; 95% CI, 1.12–1.96) is compatible with that pooled estimate after accounting for age, sex, Charlson Comorbidity Index, prior hospitalization, and index antibiotic class. Freedberg et al reported an association between PPI use and recurrent CDI in 2015 [DATA NEEDED: study design, cohort size, and effect estimate with 95% CI from Freedberg et al]. Our cohort examined a distinct clinical context: 90-day post-discharge CDI among older adults hospitalized with community-acquired pneumonia, in whom home PPI use was common (2377 of 6214 patients) [CITATION NEEDED: any prior studies examining PPI use and CDI specifically in patients with community-acquired pneumonia]. Biologically, acid suppression raises gastric pH, which may allow ingested vegetative *C difficile* cells to survive transit or alter the intestinal microbiota after antibiotic exposure [CITATION NEEDED: mechanistic studies on PPIs, gastric pH, and gut microbiota disruption].

### Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, data came from a single academic health system, which limits generalizability to community hospitals or regions with different prescribing patterns and local *C difficile* epidemiology. Second, PPI exposure was ascertained only from admission medication reconciliation, without information on duration of prior use, adherence, or whether therapy was started or stopped during or after the index hospitalization. Nondifferential exposure misclassification would generally bias the hazard ratio toward the null, although unmeasured changes in PPI use after admission could shift the estimate in either direction. Third, 312 patients (5.0%) were excluded because admission medication reconciliation was missing. If missingness was related to both PPI use and CDI risk, these exclusions could introduce selection bias in an uncertain direction. Fourth, CDI was ascertained only from inpatient and outpatient laboratory records within our health system; cases diagnosed at outside facilities were missed, which underestimates absolute 90-day CDI incidence and could bias the hazard ratio if out-of-system care differed between PPI users and nonusers. Finally, residual confounding may persist despite adjustment for age, sex, Charlson Comorbidity Index, prior hospitalization, and index antibiotic class. Unmeasured differences in pneumonia severity, frailty, post-discharge antibiotic exposure, or prior CDI history could explain part of the observed association and would bias the hazard ratio away from the null if those factors were more common among PPI users.
