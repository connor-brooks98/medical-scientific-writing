## Results

Among 6,214 adults aged 65 years or older hospitalized with community-acquired pneumonia, 2,377 were using a proton pump inhibitor (PPI) at admission and 3,837 were not. Within 90 days after discharge, *Clostridioides difficile* infection (CDI) was identified in 4.1% of PPI users and 2.6% of nonusers (unadjusted absolute difference, 1.5 percentage points). PPI use was associated with a higher hazard of CDI after adjustment for age, sex, Charlson Comorbidity Index, hospitalization in the preceding 12 months, and antibiotic class during the index hospitalization (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR], 1.48; 95% CI, 1.12-1.96). In prespecified analyses, the aHR for CDI was 1.81 (95% CI, 1.24-2.64) among men and 1.21 (95% CI, 0.81-1.80) among women; the interaction test did not provide clear evidence that the association differed by sex (*P* for interaction = .14). All-cause mortality at 90 days was 11.8% among PPI users and 11.2% among nonusers (aHR, 1.03; 95% CI, 0.88-1.21). Readmission at 30 days occurred in 17.9% of PPI users and 16.4% of nonusers (aHR, 1.06; 95% CI, 0.94-1.20).

## Discussion

In this retrospective cohort of older adults hospitalized with community-acquired pneumonia, home PPI use at admission was associated with a higher hazard of CDI during the 90 days after discharge. The association persisted after adjustment for comorbidity burden, prior hospitalization, and antibiotic class. In contrast, adjusted estimates for 90-day mortality and 30-day readmission were close to the null, although their confidence intervals did not exclude modest differences in either direction.

The CDI finding is consistent with the meta-analysis by Kwok et al,¹ who reported higher odds of CDI among PPI users (pooled odds ratio, 1.74). The present study identifies an association in a specific clinical setting: the transition from hospital to home or postacute care after an admission for community-acquired pneumonia. Medication reconciliation during that admission offers an opportunity to review whether a PPI has an ongoing indication. These data do not establish that discontinuing PPI therapy would reduce postdischarge CDI.

## Limitations

This study has several limitations. Medication reconciliation captured home PPI use only at admission; duration, adherence, and continuation after discharge were unknown. Exclusion of 312 patients with missing medication reconciliation data may have introduced selection bias. Residual confounding is also possible, particularly by the indication for PPI therapy and by antibiotic duration, which was not captured by adjustment for antibiotic class. CDI ascertainment was limited to inpatient and outpatient laboratory testing within the health system, so infections diagnosed elsewhere may have been missed. If PPI users and nonusers differed in their likelihood of returning to the system for testing, this could have biased the association. Finally, findings from a single academic health system may not generalize to other settings.
