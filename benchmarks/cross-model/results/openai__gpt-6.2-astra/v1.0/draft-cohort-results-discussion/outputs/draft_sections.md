## Results

The analytic cohort included 6,214 adults aged 65 years or older hospitalized with community-acquired pneumonia: 2,377 used a proton pump inhibitor (PPI) at home at admission and 3,837 did not. Within 90 days after discharge, *Clostridioides difficile* infection (CDI) was documented in 4.1% of PPI users and 2.6% of nonusers. PPI use was associated with a higher hazard of CDI after adjustment for age, sex, Charlson Comorbidity Index, hospitalization in the preceding 12 months, and antibiotic class during the index stay (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR], 1.48; 95% CI, 1.12-1.96). In the prespecified analysis by sex, the aHR was 1.81 (95% CI, 1.24-2.64) in men and 1.21 (95% CI, 0.81-1.80) in women (P = .14 for interaction). All-cause mortality at 90 days was 11.8% in PPI users and 11.2% in nonusers (aHR, 1.03; 95% CI, 0.88-1.21). Readmission at 30 days occurred in 17.9% of PPI users and 16.4% of nonusers (aHR, 1.06; 95% CI, 0.94-1.20).

## Discussion

Among older adults hospitalized with community-acquired pneumonia in a single academic health system from 2016 to 2023, home PPI use at admission was associated with a higher adjusted hazard of CDI within 90 days after discharge. The observed absolute difference was 1.5 percentage points before adjustment. We found no clear association with 90-day all-cause mortality or 30-day readmission. Although the CDI point estimate was higher in men than in women, the interaction test did not support a conclusion that the association differed by sex.

The direction of the association is consistent with the 2012 meta-analysis by Kwok et al, who reported higher odds of CDI among PPI users (pooled odds ratio, approximately 1.74). The hazard ratio in our cohort describes CDI during a defined postdischarge interval and is not directly comparable with the pooled odds ratio. For a clinician reviewing medications at discharge, a separate question is whether discontinuing a PPI would lower CDI risk. Our comparison was based on home use at admission, not on whether patients continued or discontinued treatment at discharge.

## Limitations

Medication reconciliation at admission did not establish the duration of PPI use, adherence, or exposure after discharge. We excluded 312 patients who lacked medication reconciliation data; this exclusion could bias the estimate if the availability of these data was associated with both PPI use and CDI risk. We ascertained CDI from inpatient and outpatient laboratory results within the health system and therefore could not identify infections diagnosed only elsewhere. If PPI users were more likely than nonusers to return to the system for testing, the observed association could be overstated. Residual confounding by the indication for PPI therapy or by differences in antibiotic exposure not captured by drug class could also account for part of the association. Findings from one academic health system may not apply to older adults receiving care elsewhere.
