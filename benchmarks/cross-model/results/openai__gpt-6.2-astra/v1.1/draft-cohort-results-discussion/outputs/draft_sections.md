## Results

Among 6214 adults aged 65 years or older hospitalized with community-acquired pneumonia, 2377 used a proton pump inhibitor (PPI) at home at the time of admission and 3837 did not. Within 90 days after discharge, *Clostridioides difficile* infection (CDI) was detected in 4.1% of PPI users and 2.6% of nonusers. The adjusted hazard ratio (aHR) was 1.48 (95% CI, 1.12–1.96) after adjustment for age, sex, Charlson Comorbidity Index, hospitalization in the preceding 12 months, and antibiotic class during the index stay. For PPI users and nonusers, respectively, 90-day all-cause mortality was 11.8% and 11.2% (aHR, 1.03; 95% CI, 0.88–1.21), and 30-day readmission was 17.9% and 16.4% (aHR, 1.06; 95% CI, 0.94–1.20). In the prespecified subgroup analysis, the aHR for CDI was 1.81 (95% CI, 1.24–2.64) in men and 1.21 (95% CI, 0.81–1.80) in women. The interaction test did not provide evidence that the association differed by sex (*P* for interaction = .14).

## Discussion

In this retrospective cohort of older adults hospitalized with community-acquired pneumonia, home PPI use at admission was associated with a higher hazard of CDI in the 90 days after discharge. The observed difference in CDI risk was 1.5 percentage points before adjustment. We found no clear association between PPI use and either 90-day all-cause mortality or 30-day readmission.

The direction of the CDI association was consistent with the 2012 meta-analysis by Kwok et al, which reported higher odds of CDI among PPI users (pooled odds ratio, approximately 1.74). [CITATION NEEDED: verify the Kwok et al reference and exact pooled estimate.] The present cohort addresses a specific clinical interval: the 90 days after hospitalization for pneumonia. Although the association persisted after adjustment for antibiotic class during hospitalization, PPI use was recorded only at admission. These data therefore cannot establish whether stopping a PPI at discharge would alter subsequent CDI risk.

## Limitations

Medication reconciliation identified home PPI use but did not establish duration, adherence, or continued use after discharge. Residual confounding is possible if the indication for PPI therapy or other differences between users and nonusers were associated with CDI risk. We ascertained CDI from inpatient and outpatient laboratory records within one academic health system. Infections diagnosed elsewhere were missed, and differences between groups in where patients sought care could bias the association in either direction. Excluding 312 patients with missing medication reconciliation could also introduce selection bias if these patients differed in both PPI use and CDI risk. Findings from this single system during 2016–2023 may not generalize to other settings.
