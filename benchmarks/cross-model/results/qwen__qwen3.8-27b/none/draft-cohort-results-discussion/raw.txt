# Draft Sections

## Results

In this retrospective cohort of 6,214 adults aged 65 years or older admitted to a single academic health system with a diagnosis of community-acquired pneumonia from 2016 through 2023, documented home proton pump inhibitor (PPI) use at admission was associated with a higher incidence of *C. difficile* infection (CDI) within 90 days of discharge. The primary outcome occurred in 4.1% of PPI users (n = 2,377) compared with 2.6% of nonusers (n = 3,837), an absolute difference of 1.5 percentage points. After adjustment for age, sex, Charlson comorbidity index, prior hospitalization in the previous 12 months, and antibiotic class during the index admission, PPI use was associated with a 48% higher hazard of CDI (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR], 1.48; 95% CI, 1.12-1.96) in a Cox proportional hazards model. For the prespecified secondary outcomes, there were no statistically significant associations with 90-day all-cause mortality (11.8% vs 11.2%; aHR, 1.03; 95% CI, 0.88-1.21) or 30-day readmission (17.9% vs 16.4%; aHR, 1.06; 95% CI, 0.94-1.20). In prespecified sex-stratified analyses, the association with CDI was stronger in men (aHR, 1.81; 95% CI, 1.24-2.64) than in women (aHR, 1.21; 95% CI, 0.81-1.80), but the interaction term was not statistically significant (P = .14).

## Discussion

In this cohort of older adults hospitalized with community-acquired pneumonia, documented home PPI use at admission was associated with an increased hazard of *C. difficile* infection within 90 days of discharge. The adjusted hazard ratio of 1.48 (95% CI, 1.12-1.96) is directionally consistent with prior literature, including a 2012 meta-analysis by Kwok et al (Am J Gastroenterol. 2012) that reported an odds ratio of approximately 1.74 for PPI use and CDI, and Freedberg et al (Clin Infect Dis. 2015), who found PPI use associated with recurrent CDI. The finding is particularly relevant in an older adult population in whom PPIs are commonly used, where the index illness and its treatment may already create vulnerability to CDI, and where subsequent CDI episodes can trigger substantial morbidity and healthcare utilization. Because this was an observational cohort with exposure captured only at admission, the result supports a consistent association but does not establish that PPI use caused the infections.

The lack of statistically significant associations with 90-day all-cause mortality and 30-day readmission may reflect the limited ability of these relatively short-term outcomes to capture the full impact of CDI, competing risks related to serious pneumonia, or insufficient power. The sex-stratified results, showing a larger point estimate in men than in women, are biologically plausible and may reflect sex differences in gastric acidity, microbiome composition, comorbidity burden, or PPI use patterns; however, the nonsignificant interaction and single-site design preclude strong subgroup conclusions. Clinically, the findings should prompt careful reevaluation of PPI indications in older adults admitted with pneumonia, particularly when therapy is being continued without a clear indication, but they do not support reflexive discontinuation of PPIs without individualized assessment.

## Limitations

Several limitations should be considered. The study was conducted in a single academic health system, which may limit generalizability to community hospitals or other settings. Home PPI exposure was ascertained only at admission from the medication record; therefore, dose, duration, adherence, indication, and changes in PPI therapy during or after hospitalization were unknown, introducing possible exposure misclassification. *C. difficile* infection was ascertained only through inpatient and outpatient laboratory testing within our system, so infections diagnosed outside the system may have been missed, potentially biasing the results in an unknown direction. In addition, 312 patients (5.0%) were excluded because of missing medication records, and the reasons for missing data may have been related to clinical status or PPI use. As a retrospective observational study, the analysis was subject to residual confounding and indication bias; PPI use may be associated with gastrointestinal disorders, frailty, or other factors that were not fully captured by the adjusted covariates. Finally, the prespecified sex subgroup analysis was not powered to detect effect modification, and the interaction term was not statistically significant, so the sex-specific estimates should be interpreted cautiously.
