Introduction

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is the most common sustained cardiac arrhythmia and a major contributor to cardiovascular morbidity and mortality worldwide. It is associated with an increased risk of stroke, heart failure, and death, and its prevalence rises steeply with advancing age. Because AF is frequently asymptomatic or presents with nonspecific symptoms, it is often undiagnosed until a complication occurs, making systematic screening an attractive public-health strategy.

Wearable devices have emerged as potential tools for arrhythmia detection, particularly for rhythm monitoring outside the clinic. Smartwatches that use photoplethysmography (PPG) and, in some models, single-lead electrocardiography (ECG) have shown promising sensitivity and specificity for detecting AF in previous studies. Earlier identification may also allow timely clinical intervention, including anticoagulation and rhythm or rate control, potentially reducing stroke risk.

However, evidence in older adults remains limited, despite this population bearing a disproportionate burden of AF and being at highest risk for complications. Older adults are also more likely to have conditions that may affect wearable signal quality or interpretation, including tremor, peripheral vascular disease, skin changes, arrhythmias, and frequent ectopy. These factors may reduce device accuracy or generate false positives, making population-specific validation essential before such technologies are used in routine clinical care.

To address this gap, we conducted a prospective diagnostic accuracy study to evaluate the performance of a commercially available smartwatch for detecting AF in adults aged 75 years and older. A 14-day continuous patch electrocardiogram was used as the reference standard to define AF presence and adjudicate smartwatch detections. This study provides direct evidence on the diagnostic accuracy of smartwatch-based AF detection in a clinically important older adult population and may inform the appropriate use of wearable technology in future screening and management strategies.
