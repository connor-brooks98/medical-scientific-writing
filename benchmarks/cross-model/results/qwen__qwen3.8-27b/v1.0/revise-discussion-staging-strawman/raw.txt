## Discussion

The central question was whether early mobilization is safe after hip fracture surgery in patients with dementia. In this retrospective cohort of 1,146 patients with dementia, mobilization within 24 hours of surgery was associated with lower 30-day mortality (6.2% vs 9.8%; adjusted odds ratio, 0.61; 95% CI, 0.39–0.95) and shorter length of stay (median 8 vs 11 days).

The association does not mean that delayed mobilization is always harmful. Delirium incidence did not differ between groups (31% vs 29%). The absence of a difference does not mean that early mobilization has no cognitive effects; our study could not detect them.

The table below compares our findings with previous cohorts. Early mobilization was feasible for most patients. Our findings suggest that even patients with severe dementia could participate in early physiotherapy. The results have two limitations. First, information on preoperative ambulatory status was not recorded for 18% of patients; the missing values suggest these patients were likely nonambulatory before the fracture, which limits how well we can describe baseline function. Second, the retrospective design leaves open residual confounding: even after adjustment, patients who were mobilized early may have differed in unmeasured factors such as baseline health. In this cohort of patients with dementia, early mobilization after hip fracture surgery was associated with lower 30-day mortality and shorter length of stay, with no difference in delirium. A randomized trial of early versus delayed mobilization in patients with dementia would test whether early mobilization reduces 30-day mortality.
