## Discussion

In this retrospective cohort of 1,146 patients with dementia undergoing hip fracture surgery, mobilization within 24 hours of surgery was associated with lower 30-day mortality (6.2% vs 9.8%; adjusted odds ratio, 0.61; 95% CI, 0.39–0.95) and shorter length of stay (median 8 vs 11 days). Recorded delirium incidence was similar in the two groups (31% vs 29%). These associations are consistent with a benefit of early mobilization in this population, but they should be read as hypothesis-generating. The study was framed as a question of safety, and the outcomes available here speak mainly to benefit; a safety claim would require data on mobilization-related adverse events (falls, implant-related complications, hemodynamic instability, interruption of physiotherapy), which are not presented.

The principal threat to a causal reading is confounding by indication, including reverse causation. The timing of first mobilization is not assigned at random: it is determined by hemodynamic stability, pain control, postoperative delirium, prefracture mobility, and dementia severity. Patients who are able to mobilize within 24 hours may therefore be those who were already recovering more favorably, and early mobilization may be a marker of that trajectory rather than its cause. Statistical adjustment attenuates but does not remove this problem, particularly when frailty, dementia severity, and prefracture functional status are incompletely captured. A related concern is the exposure definition itself: to be classified as mobilized within 24 hours, a patient must survive and remain stable through that window, so early deaths and early deterioration are necessarily allocated to the delayed group. This immortal-time structure can produce a mortality association in the absence of any treatment effect. Length of stay is similarly vulnerable, because death truncates the admission and acts as a competing event; the direction of the resulting bias depends on how early deaths were distributed between groups. Analyses that treat mobilization as a time-varying exposure, or that emulate a target trial with an explicit time zero, would help clarify how much of the observed difference is attributable to the intervention.

Preoperative ambulatory status was not recorded for 18% of patients. This gap should not be interpreted as evidence that these patients were nonambulatory before fracture. Documentation may be missing for reasons unrelated to function — admission pathway, transfer from another facility, urgency of surgery, or the severity of cognitive impairment — and any of these could also be related to both mobilization timing and outcome. Reclassifying missing values as nonambulatory would introduce misclassification of unknown magnitude and unpredictable direction. We therefore report this as missing information, and the analysis would be strengthened by describing missingness by group, comparing observed characteristics of patients with and without recorded status, and repeating the primary analysis under multiple imputation and explicit best-case/worst-case assumptions.

The delirium comparison warrants a narrower interpretation than the mortality and length-of-stay findings. We did not detect a statistically significant difference between groups (31% vs 29%); the precision of this comparison is not reported, and no equivalence margin was prespecified, so the result neither establishes that early mobilization is cognitively neutral nor demonstrates a hidden cognitive effect. It is an inconclusive comparison. Ascertainment is a further limitation: delirium was identified from routine documentation rather than standardized screening, hypoactive presentations are readily missed, and its features overlap with baseline dementia, all of which favor underdetection and could mask a difference in either direction. For the same reasons, this comparison cannot resolve the objection that patients with severe dementia are unable to participate in early physiotherapy. Dementia severity was not stratified, and we do not report the proportion of patients in whom mobilization was attempted versus achieved, the level of assistance required, or the reasons for deferral. A claim that early mobilization was feasible for most patients is therefore not supported by the data presented here, and feasibility in severe dementia remains an open question.

Additional limitations follow from the retrospective single-cohort design. Exposure was characterized by timing alone, without dose, duration, or intensity of mobilization, so no threshold or dose–response relationship can be inferred. Residual confounding by unmeasured frailty, nutritional status, and social and discharge-planning factors is likely, and length of stay in particular is sensitive to institutional discharge practice. Functional recovery, destination of discharge, and mortality beyond 30 days were not assessed, so the durability of any benefit is unknown. Generalizability to settings with different orthogeriatric staffing or physiotherapy availability is uncertain.

In summary, mobilization within 24 hours of hip fracture surgery was associated with lower 30-day mortality and shorter hospital stay in patients with dementia, without a detected difference in recorded delirium. Confounding by indication, the immortal-time structure of the exposure, and incomplete data on prefracture function prevent a causal conclusion, and the available outcomes do not establish safety. The findings justify prospective evaluation — ideally a randomized trial, or a carefully specified target-trial emulation — with stratification by dementia severity and prefracture mobility, standardized delirium assessment anchored to baseline cognition, and prespecified adverse-event endpoints. Until such evidence is available, these data support considering early mobilization in appropriately selected patients with dementia rather than adopting it as a uniform protocol requirement.

Here's what I think was reading off, and what I changed:

**1. Causal language on observational data.** "This illustrates the importance of early mobilization" treated an adjusted odds ratio as proof of effect. Worse, the design has two specific biases the original never named: *confounding by indication/reverse causation* (patients who can mobilize at 24 hours are the ones already recovering well) and *immortal time* (you must survive the first 24 hours to be counted as "early," so early deaths land in the delayed group by construction). Either could generate the mortality finding on its own. I added both, plus death as a competing event for length of stay.

**2. The missing-data inference was backwards.** "Not recorded for 18%... suggesting that these patients were likely nonambulatory" converts missingness into a clinical finding. Documentation gaps track admission route, transfers, and dementia severity — not function. I reframed it as missing information and suggested imputation plus best/worst-case sensitivity analyses.
