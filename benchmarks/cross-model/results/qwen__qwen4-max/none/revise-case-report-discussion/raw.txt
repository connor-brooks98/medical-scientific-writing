## Discussion

Euglycemic diabetic ketoacidosis (euDKA) is conventionally defined as ketoacidosis occurring with a plasma glucose concentration below 250 mg/dL. Our patient met this definition: capillary glucose was 182 mg/dL at the onset of symptoms, while venous pH was 7.12, bicarbonate 9 mmol/L, anion gap 24 mmol/L, and serum beta-hydroxybutyrate 6.8 mmol/L. The lactate of 1.4 mmol/L argues against lactic acidosis as the source of the elevated anion gap, an important consideration in a patient taking metformin, and the beta-hydroxybutyrate concentration establishes ketosis as the cause. The near-normal glucose is what made this presentation easy to misread.

SGLT2 inhibitors lower plasma glucose through glycosuria, which reduces endogenous insulin secretion and, in treated patients, often reduces exogenous insulin requirements. The resulting shift toward lipolysis and ketogenesis is usually compensated, but it leaves little reserve when carbohydrate intake falls. In the perioperative period several of these pressures act together: our patient fasted for 14 hours before surgery, underwent the counter-regulatory stress of an operation, and then had reduced oral intake complicated by nausea and vomiting on POD 1. Observational data have reported up to a three-fold increase in DKA risk among patients receiving SGLT2 inhibitors, and the perioperative setting is where that risk is most consistently described.

The more instructive feature of this case is the delay in diagnosis. Nausea, vomiting, and abdominal discomfort on the first day after laparoscopic cholecystectomy are common and were reasonably attributed to postoperative ileus and opioid analgesia. Because the capillary glucose was 182 mg/dL, nothing in the routine postoperative glucose record prompted concern. Tachypnea at 28/min, representing respiratory compensation for a metabolic acidosis, was the finding that finally prompted a blood gas, and approximately 14 hours elapsed between that first abnormal gas and the diagnosis of euDKA on the morning of POD 2. Glucose-based monitoring cannot detect this condition. In a patient taking an SGLT2 inhibitor who develops postoperative nausea, vomiting, malaise, or unexplained tachypnea, measurement of beta-hydroxybutyrate and a blood gas — rather than a repeat glucose check — should be the default response, even when the surgical explanation for the symptoms appears adequate.

Current FDA labeling recommends discontinuing empagliflozin at least 3 days before scheduled surgery [5]. Our patient took her last dose on the morning before the operation, well inside that window, and this is the most plausible modifiable contributor to her course. The case also illustrates a practical problem: the preoperative instruction addressed metformin explicitly but left empagliflozin running, and responsibility for holding it was not clearly assigned among the surgical, anesthesia, and prescribing teams. Whether an elective procedure should proceed when the recommended washout has not been observed is a decision worth making explicitly rather than by default.

Management followed the standard approach for euDKA. Because glucose was not markedly elevated, insulin infusion had to be paired with concurrent 10% dextrose to permit the insulin delivery needed to suppress ketogenesis without producing hypoglycemia, alongside fluid resuscitation and potassium replacement. The anion gap closed after 38 hours, a longer interval than is typical for hyperglycemic DKA and a reminder that the metabolic effect of these agents does not end with the last dose. Empagliflozin was discontinued permanently and she was discharged on POD 6 on metformin and basal insulin.

This report has the usual limitations of a single case. The temporal relationship between empagliflozin and the ketoacidotic episode is suggestive but does not establish causation, and the relative contributions of the drug, the prolonged fast, and surgical stress cannot be separated in one patient. The diagnosis was also delayed, so the earliest point at which ketosis was detectable is unknown.

## Conclusion

Ketoacidosis developed in this patient after laparoscopic cholecystectomy while glucose remained below 200 mg/dL, and the diagnosis was delayed by roughly 14 hours after the first abnormal blood gas because her symptoms fit a common postoperative explanation. In patients taking SGLT2 inhibitors, a normal or near-normal glucose does not exclude DKA, and postoperative nausea, vomiting, or tachypnea warrants ketone measurement and a blood gas. Adherence to the recommended 3-day preoperative discontinuation of empagliflozin, with explicit assignment of responsibility for that hold, is the most direct preventive measure available.
