Importance: Short sleep has been associated with cardiovascular disease, but whether it is associated with incident atrial fibrillation (AF) in older adults is unclear.

Objective: To examine whether self-reported sleep duration is associated with incident AF in adults aged 65 years or older.

Design, Setting, and Participants: This prospective cohort study included 4,912 community-dwelling adults without AF at baseline, enrolled at 4 US sites between 2009 and 2012 and followed up through 2022.

Exposure: Self-reported habitual sleep duration, categorized as 6 hours or less, 7 to 8 hours (reference), and 9 hours or more.

Main Outcomes and Measures: Incident AF, ascertained from study electrocardiograms, hospital discharge codes, and Medicare claims.

Results: [ADD: mean (SD) age, n (%) women, and n in each sleep-duration category.] During a median follow-up of 9.8 years, 781 participants developed AF. Participants reporting 6 hours or less of sleep had a higher hazard of AF than those reporting 7 to 8 hours (adjusted hazard ratio, 1.29; 95% CI, 1.08–1.54); 9 hours or more was not associated with AF (adjusted hazard ratio, 1.07; 95% CI, 0.86–1.33). Adjustment for obstructive sleep apnea symptoms attenuated the short-sleep estimate (adjusted hazard ratio, 1.18; 95% CI, 0.98–1.42).

Conclusions and Relevance: In this cohort of older adults, short sleep was associated with a modestly higher hazard of incident AF, and the association was attenuated after accounting for sleep apnea symptoms. Trials would be needed to determine whether treating sleep apnea or extending sleep lowers AF risk.
