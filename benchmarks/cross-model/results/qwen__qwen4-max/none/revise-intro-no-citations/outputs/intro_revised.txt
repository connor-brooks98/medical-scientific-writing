Introduction

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is the most common sustained cardiac arrhythmia, and its prevalence rises steeply with age, reaching its highest levels in adults aged 75 years and older [REF: AF epidemiology and age-specific prevalence]. AF is associated with a substantially increased risk of ischaemic stroke, heart failure and death, and advanced age is among the strongest determinants of that risk [REF: AF outcomes and risk stratification]. Because AF is frequently paroxysmal and asymptomatic, its first clinical manifestation may be a stroke, and intermittent assessment with a single pulse check or 12-lead electrocardiogram (ECG) will miss transient episodes [REF: asymptomatic/paroxysmal AF and limitations of intermittent screening]. Guidelines accordingly endorse opportunistic screening for AF in older adults, yet the optimal screening strategy in the oldest age groups remains undefined [REF: current guideline recommendation].

Smartwatches that combine photoplethysmography (PPG)-based detection of pulse irregularity with single-lead ECG confirmation offer a scalable, low-burden approach to AF screening, and large consumer-based studies have reported high sensitivity and specificity against ECG reference standards [REF: major smartwatch validation studies and recent meta-analysis]. These cohorts, however, have been dominated by younger, self-selected participants, with mean ages well below 75 years [REF]. Accuracy estimates derived from such populations cannot be assumed to transport to the oldest adults, in whom the pre-test probability of AF, the absolute risk of stroke, and therefore the potential benefit of detection are greatest.

Several age-related factors plausibly degrade wearable performance in this group. Reduced peripheral perfusion and peripheral arterial disease, tremor and limited dexterity, age-related changes in skin properties, and a higher burden of atrial and ventricular ectopy may all reduce signal quality, increase motion artefact, and generate false-positive irregularity notifications, while also limiting the proportion of recordings that can be interpreted at all [REF: PPG signal quality determinants and ectopy-related false positives]. The clinical consequences run in both directions: false-negative results may delay diagnosis in patients at high immediate risk of stroke, whereas false-positive notifications may prompt unnecessary downstream testing, inappropriate consideration of anticoagulation, and anxiety [REF]. Moreover, the clinical value of detecting device-identified AF is itself still being established; screening reliably increases AF detection and anticoagulation initiation, but a reduction in stroke has not been consistently demonstrated [REF: AF screening trials and subclinical AF anticoagulation trials]. Robust, population-specific accuracy data are a prerequisite for resolving that question.

Establishing accuracy in this setting also requires an appropriate reference standard. Because AF in older adults is often paroxysmal and of low burden, brief or intermittent comparator recordings risk misclassifying affected participants as AF-free; prolonged continuous ECG monitoring with blinded expert adjudication is needed to limit such misclassification [REF: monitoring duration and AF detection yield]. We therefore conducted a prospective diagnostic accuracy study of a commercially available smartwatch ([DEVICE, MANUFACTURER]) for the detection of AF in adults aged 75 years and older, using 14-day continuous patch ECG monitoring as the reference standard. Our primary objective was to estimate participant-level sensitivity and specificity, with 95% confidence intervals, for the device's AF detection algorithm. Secondary objectives were to quantify the proportion of recordings that were uninterpretable or of insufficient signal quality, to examine predictive values at the observed AF prevalence in this cohort, and to explore participant and signal characteristics associated with misclassification.
