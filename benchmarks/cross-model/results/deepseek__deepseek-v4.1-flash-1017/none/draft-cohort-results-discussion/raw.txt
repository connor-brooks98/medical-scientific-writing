## Results

After exclusion of 312 patients (5.0%) for missing medication reconciliation data, the analytic cohort included 6,214 adults aged 65 years or older hospitalized with community-acquired pneumonia (CAP); 2,377 (38.3%) had home proton pump inhibitor (PPI) use at admission and 3,837 (61.7%) did not. The primary outcome, *C. difficile* infection (CDI) within 90 days of discharge, occurred in 4.1% of PPI users vs 2.6% of nonusers (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR], 1.48; 95% CI, 1.12-1.96) after adjustment for age, sex, Charlson Comorbidity Index, prior hospitalization in the preceding 12 months, and antibiotic class during the index stay in a Cox proportional hazards model. Secondary outcomes were not significantly associated with PPI use: 90-day all-cause mortality occurred in 11.8% vs 11.2% (aHR, 1.03; 95% CI, 0.88-1.21), and 30-day readmission occurred in 17.9% vs 16.4% (aHR, 1.06; 95% CI, 0.94-1.20). In prespecified subgroup analyses, the aHR for CDI was 1.81 (95% CI, 1.24-2.64) among men and 1.21 (95% CI, 0.81-1.80) among women; the test for interaction was not statistically significant (P = .14).

## Discussion

In this retrospective cohort of older adults hospitalized with CAP, home PPI use at admission was associated with a higher risk of CDI within 90 days after discharge (aHR, 1.48), whereas no significant associations were observed for 90-day all-cause mortality or 30-day readmission. These findings are consistent in direction with prior evidence linking PPI exposure to CDI, including a 2012 meta-analysis by Kwok et al that reported an OR of approximately 1.74, and with Freedberg et al, who found PPI use associated with recurrent CDI. Our results extend this literature to older adults with CAP and to the postdischarge period, while suggesting that the signal may be relatively specific to CDI rather than to broader adverse outcomes such as death or readmission.

The association between PPI use and CDI is biologically plausible because acid suppression may increase susceptibility to *C. difficile* colonization and infection. However, the observational design limits causal inference; the findings may partly reflect confounding by indication, greater comorbidity, or differential healthcare contact among PPI users. The prespecified subgroup estimate was larger among men than women, but the interaction test was not statistically significant (P = .14), and this finding should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than evidence of effect modification. If confirmed, these results would support careful medication reconciliation and targeted CDI prevention efforts among older CAP survivors, but they do not establish causality or justify changing PPI therapy solely to prevent CDI.

## Limitations

This study has several limitations. It was retrospective and observational, so residual confounding remains possible despite adjustment for age, sex, Charlson Comorbidity Index, prior hospitalization, and antibiotic class; unmeasured factors such as frailty, postdischarge antibiotic exposure, and healthcare utilization could bias the estimates. PPI exposure was ascertained from medication reconciliation at admission only, and we did not have data on dose, duration, indication, adherence, or changes during follow-up, so exposure misclassification is likely. CDI was ascertained using inpatient and outpatient laboratory results within our health system, and events diagnosed outside the system may have been missed; differential healthcare contact could also introduce surveillance bias. In addition, 312 patients (5.0%) were excluded for missing medication reconciliation data, which could affect generalizability. Finally, the study was conducted within a single academic health system among adults aged 65 years or older with CAP, so the findings may not generalize to other populations or care settings.
