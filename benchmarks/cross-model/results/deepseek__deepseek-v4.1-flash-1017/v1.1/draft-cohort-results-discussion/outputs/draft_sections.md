# Draft sections: home PPI use and 90-day CDI after pneumonia admission

Retrospective cohort, single academic health system, 2016-2023. Target journal: JAMA Network Open. Formatting follows JAMA style (hyphenated CI ranges, "vs", lowercase aHR after first definition); change if the journal's current instructions differ.

## Results

Of the patients admitted with community-acquired pneumonia between 2016 and 2023, 312 (5.0%) were excluded because a medication record was unavailable; 6,214 adults aged 65 years or older remained for analysis. Of these, 2,377 were taking a proton pump inhibitor (PPI) at admission and 3,837 were not. *Clostridioides difficile* infection (CDI) within 90 days of discharge occurred in 4.1% of PPI users and 2.6% of nonusers (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR], 1.48; 95% CI, 1.12-1.96). Models were adjusted for age, sex, Charlson Comorbidity Index, hospitalization in the prior 12 months, and antibiotic class during the index stay. Neither secondary outcome differed between groups: 90-day all-cause mortality was 11.8% vs 11.2% (aHR, 1.03; 95% CI, 0.88-1.21), and 30-day readmission was 17.9% vs 16.4% (aHR, 1.06; 95% CI, 0.94-1.20). In prespecified subgroup analyses, the association between PPI use and CDI was larger among men (aHR, 1.81; 95% CI, 1.24-2.64) than among women (aHR, 1.21; 95% CI, 0.81-1.80); the interaction test was not statistically significant (P = 0.14).

## Discussion

In this cohort of 6,214 older adults hospitalized with community-acquired pneumonia, PPI use at admission was associated with a higher rate of CDI in the 90 days after discharge (aHR, 1.48; 95% CI, 1.12-1.96). Our estimate is smaller than the pooled odds ratio of 1.74 reported by Kwok et al for PPI use and CDI [1]. Adjustment for the antibiotic class given during the index stay may account for part of the difference, since antibiotic exposure is itself a strong risk factor for CDI; differences in the outcome window and in the study population may account for the rest. Freedberg et al reported an association between PPI use and recurrent CDI [2]; our results extend that observation to CDI occurring after a pneumonia admission in older adults.

Suppression of gastric acid is a plausible mechanism. PPIs raise intragastric pH, and the acid barrier is thought to limit infection by ingested *C. difficile* spores [CITATION NEEDED: mechanism]. An alternative explanation is confounding by indication: patients taking a PPI may be frailer or have more contact with the health system than nonusers, and adjustment for the Charlson Comorbidity Index and prior hospitalization does not capture all of these differences. In absolute terms the difference was 1.5 percentage points (4.1% vs 2.6%), and neither mortality nor readmission differed. The apparent difference by sex (aHR, 1.81 in men vs 1.21 in women) was not supported by the interaction test (P = 0.14), and we read it as an imprecise subgroup estimate rather than confirmed effect modification.

### Limitations

Our findings have several limitations. Exposure came from the medication history at admission, so we did not capture dose, duration, adherence, or over-the-counter PPI use, and patients who stopped a PPI before admission or started one afterward were classified as unexposed. This misclassification is likely to be nondifferential and would bias the hazard ratio toward 1. CDI was ascertained from inpatient and outpatient laboratory results within our health system, so cases diagnosed elsewhere were missed; if PPI users had more contact with the system, that difference in detection would bias the estimate away from 1. Adjustment was limited to age, sex, Charlson Comorbidity Index, prior hospitalization, and antibiotic class during the index stay, and residual confounding by frailty, functional status, or antibiotic exposure after discharge is possible. We excluded 312 patients (5.0%) whose medication records were missing, and their CDI risk is unknown. The cohort came from one health system and was restricted to adults 65 years or older admitted with pneumonia, so the results may not generalize to other systems or to younger adults.
