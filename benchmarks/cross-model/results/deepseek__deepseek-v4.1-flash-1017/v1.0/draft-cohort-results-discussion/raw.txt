## Results

Of 6,214 adults aged 65 years or older admitted with community-acquired pneumonia between 2016 and 2023, 2,377 were taking a proton pump inhibitor (PPI) at admission; 3,837 were not. *Clostridioides difficile* infection (CDI) within 90 days of discharge occurred in 4.1% of PPI users and 2.6% of nonusers (absolute difference, 1.5 percentage points; adjusted HR, 1.48; 95% CI, 1.12-1.96). Models were adjusted for age, sex, Charlson Comorbidity Index, hospitalization in the previous 12 months, and antibiotic class during the index admission. Ninety-day all-cause mortality was 11.8% among PPI users vs 11.2% among nonusers (adjusted HR, 1.03; 95% CI, 0.88-1.21). Thirty-day readmission was 17.9% vs 16.4% (adjusted HR, 1.06; 95% CI, 0.94-1.20). In prespecified subgroup analyses, the adjusted HR for CDI was 1.81 (95% CI, 1.24-2.64) among men and 1.21 (95% CI, 0.81-1.80) among women (P for interaction = 0.14).

## Discussion

In this cohort of 6,214 older adults hospitalized with community-acquired pneumonia, PPI use at admission was associated with a higher rate of CDI within 90 days of discharge (adjusted HR, 1.48; 95% CI, 1.12-1.96). Ninety-day mortality and 30-day readmission were similar in the two groups. The size of the CDI estimate is close to the pooled OR of 1.74 reported by Kwok et al in a 2012 meta-analysis of PPI use and CDI [CITATION NEEDED: 95% CI and number of included studies]. Freedberg et al subsequently reported an association between PPI use and recurrent CDI (ref). Our outcome counted any *C difficile*-positive result in the 90 days after discharge, which includes episodes identified after discharge that inpatient-only surveillance would miss.

Reduced gastric acidity is the usual explanation offered for a higher CDI risk with PPIs. Acid suppression allows ingested *C difficile* spores to survive passage through the stomach, and it may change the composition of the gut microbiota that resists colonization. Our data are consistent with that pathway, but they cannot separate it from confounding by indication, because PPIs are prescribed for conditions that track with comorbidity, frailty, and health care contact. Mortality and readmission did not differ between groups, which makes it less likely that the CDI association reflects greater acute illness severity alone; it does not exclude that explanation. The CDI hazard ratio was larger in men (1.81; 95% CI, 1.24-2.64) than in women (1.21; 95% CI, 0.81-1.80), but the interaction P value was 0.14 and the study was not powered to detect effect modification, so we do not interpret this as a difference by sex.

## Limitations

This study has limitations. Exposure came from the medication history at admission. We did not capture dose, duration, adherence, or PPI starts and stops during the admission or after discharge, and PPIs purchased over the counter would not appear in the record at all. Misclassification of a binary exposure of this kind is usually nondifferential and would bias the hazard ratio toward the null. CDI was ascertained from inpatient and outpatient microbiology within our system only, so episodes diagnosed elsewhere are missing from the data. If PPI users had more contact with the system, differential testing would inflate the association; equal out-of-system testing in both groups would attenuate it. We could not adjust for the indication for PPI therapy, for frailty, or for antibiotic exposure after discharge. If PPI users were sicker in ways the Charlson index does not capture, that confounding would bias the estimate away from the null. A medication history was unavailable for 312 patients (5.0%), who were excluded. The cohort also came from a single academic health system and was restricted to adults 65 years or older admitted with pneumonia, so the estimates apply to that setting.
