Importance: Short sleep has been associated with cardiovascular disease, but whether it is related to new atrial fibrillation (AF) in older adults remains unclear.

Objective: To determine the association of self-reported sleep duration with incident AF in adults aged 65 years or older.

Design, Setting, and Participants: Prospective cohort study of 4,912 community-dwelling adults without AF at baseline, enrolled at 4 US sites between 2009 and 2012 and followed through 2022.

Exposure: Self-reported habitual sleep duration, categorized as 6 hours or less, 7 to 8 hours (reference), and 9 hours or more.

Main Outcomes and Measures: Incident AF, ascertained from study electrocardiograms, hospital discharge codes, and Medicare claims.

Results: During a median follow-up of 9.8 years, 781 participants developed AF. Compared with 7 to 8 hours, sleeping 6 hours or less was associated with a higher hazard of AF (adjusted hazard ratio, 1.29; 95% CI, 1.08–1.54). Sleeping 9 hours or more was not associated with AF (adjusted hazard ratio, 1.07; 95% CI, 0.86–1.33). After further adjustment for obstructive sleep apnea symptoms, the short-sleep estimate was smaller and the confidence interval included 1 (adjusted hazard ratio, 1.18; 95% CI, 0.98–1.42).

Conclusions and Relevance: Short sleep was associated with incident AF in this cohort of older adults, although the association was attenuated after accounting for sleep apnea symptoms. Trials would be needed to test whether treating sleep apnea or increasing sleep duration reduces AF risk.
