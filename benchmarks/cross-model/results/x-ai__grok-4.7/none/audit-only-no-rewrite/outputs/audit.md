# Audit: introduction.txt

Scope: diagnosis only. No rewrite. Quotes are from the draft so you can find the spots; they are not suggested replacements.

## Bottom line

The draft does not yet make a coherent case for the study. It asserts that the question is already settled, treats one single-center association as proof that early norepinephrine prevents death, then says the new study will fill a gap it never names. A reader cannot tell what is unknown, what you will test, or why a prospective study is still needed.

Fix the logic before the prose. Polishing the clichés will not repair the argument.

## 1. Causation claimed from association (most serious)

> "norepinephrine started within 1 hour of shock onset was associated with lower 28-day mortality (adjusted odds ratio, 0.78; 95% CI, 0.64–0.94). Early norepinephrine therefore prevents death in septic shock."

- "Associated with" and "adjusted odds ratio" are association language. "Therefore prevents death" is a causal conclusion. The "therefore" does not follow.
- One retrospective, single-center analysis cannot establish that a treatment prevents death. Residual confounding, confounding by indication, and timing bias (how "shock onset" is defined, who is still eligible to receive the drug at each minute) are the usual threats here, and none is acknowledged.
- The claim is also absolute. "Prevents death" means the outcome does not occur. Even a significant mortality reduction would not justify that verb. The CI speaks to precision of an adjusted association, not to proof of prevention.
- "In septic shock" universalizes a result from "our institution." Generalizability is not addressed.
- What was adjusted for is not stated, so a reader cannot judge whether the aOR is even a fair summary of the association, let alone a causal effect.
- No absolute risks, event counts, or baseline mortality are given, so clinical importance is not shown—only a relative association.

This sentence will be the one reviewers attack first. Do not leave a causal verb standing on observational data.

## 2. The "gap" is undefined, and the draft contradicts it

> "To address this critical gap, we designed a comprehensive prospective study that will not only fill an important gap in the literature..."

- "This critical gap" has no antecedent. Nothing before it states what is unknown.
- The preceding sentences say studies have shown a survival benefit, your own data confirm lower mortality, and early norepinephrine prevents death. If that were true, there would be no gap to fill, and the rationale for another study would be unclear (including why it would be ethical to withhold a treatment you say prevents death).
- "Address this critical gap" and "fill an important gap" say the same thing twice and still do not say what the gap is.
- "Pave the way for improved outcomes" is a hope, not an objective. It does not name population, exposure or intervention, comparator, outcome, or hypothesis.

By the end of the introduction, a reader should know what you will do and why it is not already answered. This draft does neither.

## 3. The certainty of the claims does not match across sentences

Three neighboring claims, three different strengths:

1. "Studies have shown that early norepinephrine improves survival in septic shock." — cited as established fact; causal; no citations.
2. "delayed vasopressor therapy may potentially be associated with worse outcomes in some patients." — five hedges ("suggests," "may," "potentially," "associated," "some patients").
3. "Early norepinephrine therefore prevents death in septic shock." — stronger than either of the above.

Delayed harm is roughly the inverse of early benefit. If you believe (1), (2) should not be timid; if you believe (2), (1) and (3) are too strong. As written, the paragraph does not have a stable position on the evidence.

Also, (1) is unsupported in the text. "Studies have shown" needs citations, and "improves survival" is a high bar. Do not state it as settled unless the sources you cite actually show a mortality benefit rather than faster shock resolution or an adjusted association. If the literature is mixed or mostly observational, the sentence overclaims—and that overclaim is what makes the later "gap" incoherent.

## 4. Argument structure

What the two paragraphs currently do:

- Paragraph 1: broad importance of sepsis → vague "growing interest" → unsourced survival claim → hedged restatement of the same idea.
- Paragraph 2: one local association → causal leap → unnamed gap → unnamed prospective study.

What is missing for a clinical introduction:

- A specific clinical problem (for example, what is uncertain about timing, dose, fluids, or which patients), not "sepsis is important."
- A clean known-versus-unknown split. Right now "known" is overstated and "unknown" is absent.
- Why existing studies do not answer the question this prospective study asks.
- What the retrospective analysis is doing in the introduction: published prior work, hypothesis-generating data, or a result of this paper. Those are different, and the draft treats it as proof.
- A last sentence that states the study question. "We designed a comprehensive prospective study" is a process statement, not an aim.

"Shock onset," "early," and "vasopressor" versus "norepinephrine" are used without definition. "Early" only becomes "within 1 hour" in the results sentence, and it is unclear whether that threshold is the study's hypothesis or a cut-point from the retrospective analysis.

## 5. Redundancy

- Sentence 3 (early norepinephrine improves survival) and sentence 4 (delayed therapy may be associated with worse outcomes) are the same claim from opposite directions. One of them is not earning its place—and they disagree in confidence (see §3).
- "Growing interest" and "a growing body of evidence" are the same gesture.
- "Address this critical gap" / "fill an important gap" is a loop.
- "In recent years" adds no information.

## 6. Empty phrasing

These phrases sound like significance but do not specify a mechanism, a population, or a result:

- "pivotal challenge in the evolving landscape of critical care"
- "growing interest in the role of"
- "growing body of evidence"
- "critical gap" / "important gap"
- "comprehensive prospective study"
- "pave the way for improved outcomes"
- "not only ... but also" joining two vague benefits

"May potentially be associated ... in some patients" is stacked hedging. It reads as caution, but it removes the claim. Either the evidence supports a qualified association you can state once, or it does not belong as a pillar of the introduction.

"Furthermore" implies a new point. The next sentence is not one.

## 7. How the numbers are used

The aOR and CI are the only concrete evidence in the draft, and they are asked to do too much.

- A CI that excludes 1 supports a statistically significant adjusted association in this dataset. It does not identify the causal factor, rule out bias, or show that deaths were prevented.
- Without the covariates, the mortality in each timing group, and how time zero was set, the number cannot carry the paragraph.
- If this analysis is preliminary or unadjusted for the main confounders, leading with it as the bridge to "prevents death" makes the overclaim worse, not better.

Do not invent stronger language around this result in the rewrite. Match the verb to the design.

## Priority for your rewrite

1. Delete or recast "therefore prevents death." Keep the claim inside what a retrospective association can support, or move that analysis out of the rationale.
2. State the actual gap in one sentence: what is already shown, what is not, and why that matters clinically. Do not call it critical until you have named it.
3. Make the evidence language consistent. Do not say the literature has shown a survival benefit and then hedge the same idea into meaninglessness.
4. End on a specific aim (population, comparison, outcome), not on "comprehensive" or "pave the way."
5. Only after that, cut the landscape/interest/gap boilerplate. Those lines are symptoms; the broken argument is the disease.

## What this audit did not do

- No revised introduction, no suggested sentences, no line edit you could paste in.
- No literature verdict on whether early norepinephrine reduces mortality. The draft's problem is that it asserts that verdict without citations and then contradicts it. Check the papers you mean to cite before you keep "improves survival."
- The odds ratio, interval, and sample size were taken as yours. The issue is interpretation, not arithmetic.
