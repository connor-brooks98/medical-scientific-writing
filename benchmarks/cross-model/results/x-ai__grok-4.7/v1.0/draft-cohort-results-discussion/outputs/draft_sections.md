## Results

The cohort included 6,214 adults aged 65 years or older admitted with community-acquired pneumonia to one academic health system from 2016 through 2023. Of these, 2,377 had home proton pump inhibitor (PPI) use documented on the admission medication reconciliation and 3,837 did not. *Clostridioides difficile* infection (CDI) within 90 days after discharge occurred in 4.1% of PPI users and 2.6% of non-users. In a Cox model adjusted for age, sex, Charlson index, hospitalization in the prior 12 months, and antibiotic class during the index stay, home PPI use was associated with CDI (adjusted hazard ratio [HR], 1.48; 95% CI, 1.12-1.96). It was not associated with 90-day all-cause mortality (11.8% vs 11.2%; adjusted HR, 1.03; 95% CI, 0.88-1.21) or 30-day readmission (17.9% vs 16.4%; adjusted HR, 1.06; 95% CI, 0.94-1.20). In prespecified subgroup analyses, the adjusted HR for CDI was 1.81 (95% CI, 1.24-2.64) in men and 1.21 (95% CI, 0.81-1.80) in women (interaction P = 0.14). Those subgroup results do not establish a difference by sex.

## Discussion

Home PPI use, recorded at admission, was associated with CDI within 90 days after discharge among 6,214 adults aged 65 years or older hospitalized with community-acquired pneumonia (adjusted HR, 1.48; 95% CI, 1.12-1.96). The same exposure was not associated with 90-day all-cause mortality or 30-day readmission. Patients were not randomized to a PPI, so this hazard ratio should not be interpreted as the effect of starting or stopping one.

Kwok et al, in a 2012 meta-analysis, reported a pooled odds ratio of approximately 1.74 for PPI use and CDI (1). The estimate here is an adjusted hazard ratio of 1.48 in adults aged 65 years or older after hospitalization for community-acquired pneumonia. The two results agree in direction only; the designs, populations, and effect measures differ. Freedberg et al reported an association between PPI use and recurrent CDI (2), which this study did not measure.

## Limitations

PPI exposure was defined only from the admission medication reconciliation. Duration of use and adherence were unknown, and inpatient PPI use after admission was not included in the exposure. If misclassification of PPI use was independent of CDI, it would be expected to bias the hazard ratio toward 1. CDI was ascertained from inpatient and outpatient tests in this health system alone. Infections diagnosed elsewhere were not captured, and we could not determine whether outside testing differed between PPI users and non-users. We excluded 312 patients (5.0%) because medication reconciliation was missing. If missingness was related to both PPI use and CDI, that exclusion could have biased the estimate in either direction. The Cox model adjusted for age, sex, Charlson index, hospitalization in the prior 12 months, and antibiotic class, but not for PPI indication, so residual confounding remains possible. The cohort included only adults aged 65 years or older treated in one academic health system.
