## Discussion

Our patient developed euglycemic diabetic ketoacidosis (euglycemic DKA) after an uncomplicated laparoscopic cholecystectomy. Sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 (SGLT2) inhibitors, used for type 2 diabetes, heart failure, and chronic kidney disease, increase urinary glucose excretion, lower insulin requirements, and increase ketone production. Surgical stress, prolonged fasting, and reduced carbohydrate intake can precipitate ketoacidosis in patients taking these drugs, including when glucose is not markedly elevated. Euglycemic DKA associated with SGLT2 inhibitors is described as rare but potentially life-threatening [CITATION NEEDED: incidence and mortality figures for that description].

The risk of diabetic ketoacidosis has been reported as up to three-fold higher in patients receiving SGLT2 inhibitors [CITATION NEEDED: source, population, and comparator for the three-fold estimate]. The US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) recommends that empagliflozin be discontinued at least 3 days before scheduled surgery [5]. She was instructed to hold metformin on the morning of surgery, but empagliflozin was continued. Her last dose was the morning before surgery, and she fasted for 14 hours. Continuing empagliflozin until the morning before surgery likely contributed to her euglycemic DKA, together with the 14-hour fast and surgical stress. One case cannot separate those contributions.

The surgical team attributed her nausea, vomiting, and abdominal discomfort on postoperative day (POD) 1 to postoperative ileus and opioid analgesia. Capillary glucose was 182 mg/dL. That evening she was tachypneic (respiratory rate 28/min). A venous blood gas showed pH 7.12, bicarbonate 9 mmol/L, and an anion gap of 24 mmol/L, with lactate 1.4 mmol/L and serum beta-hydroxybutyrate 6.8 mmol/L. Euglycemic DKA was diagnosed on the morning of POD 2, approximately 14 hours after that gas.

After transfer to the intensive care unit, she was treated with an intravenous insulin infusion with concurrent 10% dextrose, fluid resuscitation, and potassium replacement. The anion gap closed after 38 hours. Empagliflozin was discontinued permanently, and she was discharged on POD 6 taking metformin and basal insulin. This case does not show whether earlier treatment would have changed her course.

## Conclusion

Euglycemic DKA followed elective laparoscopic cholecystectomy in a patient whose last dose of empagliflozin was the morning before surgery, rather than at least 3 days earlier as the FDA recommends [5]. Glucose was 182 mg/dL, and the diagnosis was made approximately 14 hours after the first abnormal gas. This case suggests that nausea, vomiting, or tachypnea after surgery in a patient who took empagliflozin the morning before should prompt a blood gas and a ketone measurement, even when glucose is not markedly elevated, rather than attribution to postoperative ileus and opioids alone. This case does not show whether stopping empagliflozin at least 3 days before surgery would have prevented the ketoacidosis.
