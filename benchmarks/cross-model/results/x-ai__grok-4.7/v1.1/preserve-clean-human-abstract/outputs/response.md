Importance: Short sleep has been associated with cardiovascular disease, but its relationship with incident atrial fibrillation (AF) in older adults is uncertain.

Objective: To examine whether self-reported sleep duration is associated with incident AF in adults aged 65 years or older.

Design, Setting, and Participants: This prospective cohort study used data from 4,912 community-dwelling adults without AF at baseline who were enrolled at 4 US sites between 2009 and 2012 and followed up through 2022.

Exposure: Self-reported habitual sleep duration, categorized as 6 hours or less, 7 to 8 hours (reference), and 9 hours or more.

Main Outcomes and Measures: Incident AF, ascertained from study electrocardiograms, hospital discharge codes, and Medicare claims.

Results: During a median follow-up of 9.8 years, 781 participants developed AF. Compared with 7 to 8 hours, sleeping 6 hours or less was associated with a higher hazard of AF (adjusted hazard ratio, 1.29; 95% CI, 1.08–1.54), whereas sleeping 9 hours or more was not (adjusted hazard ratio, 1.07; 95% CI, 0.86–1.33). The association for short sleep was attenuated after adjustment for obstructive sleep apnea symptoms (adjusted hazard ratio, 1.18; 95% CI, 0.98–1.42).

Conclusions and Relevance: In this cohort of older adults, short sleep was associated with incident AF, but the association was weaker after adjustment for obstructive sleep apnea symptoms. Whether treating obstructive sleep apnea or extending sleep lowers AF risk would require trials.
