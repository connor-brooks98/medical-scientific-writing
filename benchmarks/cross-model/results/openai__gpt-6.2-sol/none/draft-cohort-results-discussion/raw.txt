## Results

Among 6,214 adults aged 65 years or older hospitalized with community-acquired pneumonia, 2,377 (38.3%) had home proton pump inhibitor (PPI) use documented at admission and 3,837 (61.7%) did not. Within 90 days after discharge, *Clostridioides difficile* infection (CDI) occurred in 4.1% of PPI users and 2.6% of nonusers. In a Cox model adjusted for age, sex, Charlson Comorbidity Index, hospitalization in the preceding 12 months, and antibiotic class during the index hospitalization, PPI use was associated with a higher hazard of CDI (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR], 1.48; 95% CI, 1.12-1.96). The aHR was 1.03 (95% CI, 0.88-1.21) for 90-day all-cause mortality (11.8% vs 11.2%) and 1.06 (95% CI, 0.94-1.20) for 30-day readmission (17.9% vs 16.4%). In prespecified analyses by sex, the aHR for CDI was 1.81 (95% CI, 1.24-2.64) among men and 1.21 (95% CI, 0.81-1.80) among women (P = .14 for interaction).

## Discussion

In this retrospective cohort of older adults hospitalized with community-acquired pneumonia, home PPI use documented at admission was associated with a higher adjusted hazard of CDI within 90 days after discharge. The observed proportions differed by 1.5 percentage points. Adjusted estimates for mortality and readmission were close to the null, and their confidence intervals included 1. Although the CDI association was numerically stronger among men than among women, the interaction analysis did not provide clear evidence that the association differed by sex.

The direction of the association is consistent with the meta-analysis by Kwok et al,¹ which reported higher odds of CDI among PPI users (pooled odds ratio, approximately 1.74). The present study focused on a specific clinical setting: older adults discharged after hospitalization for community-acquired pneumonia. Its findings support reviewing whether PPIs remain indicated during medication reconciliation, but do not establish that discontinuing PPIs would reduce postdischarge CDI risk.

### Limitations

This study has several limitations. The retrospective design precludes causal inference, and residual confounding may remain, including confounding by PPI indication or by antibiotic exposure not captured by index-hospitalization antibiotic class. Home PPI use was measured only at admission through medication reconciliation; duration, adherence, and changes in use after admission could not be determined. Excluding 312 patients with missing medication reconciliation data may have introduced selection bias. CDI ascertainment relied on inpatient and outpatient laboratory records within the health system, so infections diagnosed elsewhere may have been missed. Finally, the single academic health system setting may limit generalizability.
