## Results

The analytic cohort included 6214 adults aged 65 years or older who were admitted with community-acquired pneumonia to a single academic health system from 2016 to 2023. Of these, 2377 used a proton pump inhibitor (PPI) at home at admission and 3837 did not. We excluded 312 patients with missing admission medication reconciliation. Within 90 days after discharge, *Clostridioides difficile* infection (CDI) occurred in 4.1% of PPI users and 2.6% of nonusers (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR], 1.48; 95% CI, 1.12-1.96). The Cox model adjusted for age, sex, Charlson Comorbidity Index, hospitalization in the prior 12 months, and antibiotic class during the index admission. Among PPI users and nonusers, respectively, 90-day all-cause mortality was 11.8% and 11.2% (aHR, 1.03; 95% CI, 0.88-1.21), and 30-day readmission was 17.9% and 16.4% (aHR, 1.06; 95% CI, 0.94-1.20). In the prespecified analysis by sex, the aHR for CDI was 1.81 (95% CI, 1.24-2.64) among men and 1.21 (95% CI, 0.81-1.80) among women (*P* = .14 for interaction).

## Discussion

Among older adults hospitalized with community-acquired pneumonia, home PPI use at admission was associated with a higher hazard of CDI within 90 days after discharge. The adjusted estimates for 90-day all-cause mortality and 30-day readmission were close to 1, and both confidence intervals included 1. Although the point estimate for CDI was higher among men than among women, the interaction analysis did not establish that the association differed by sex.

The direction of the CDI association was consistent with the 2012 meta-analysis by Kwok et al, which reported an odds ratio of approximately 1.74 for CDI among PPI users. That pooled odds ratio is not directly comparable with the adjusted hazard ratio in this cohort. Freedberg et al examined recurrent CDI, a different outcome from CDI after discharge for pneumonia. Because we classified PPI use at admission, our analysis does not establish whether discontinuing a PPI at discharge would lower subsequent CDI risk.

### Limitations

Admission medication reconciliation captured home PPI use at one time point and did not establish treatment duration, adherence, or whether use continued after discharge. Excluding patients with missing medication reconciliation could have biased the association if missingness was related to both PPI use and CDI risk. We ascertained CDI from inpatient and outpatient laboratory records within the health system, so we may have missed infections diagnosed elsewhere. If capture differed between PPI users and nonusers, the association could have been biased in either direction. Residual confounding by the indication for PPI use is possible, and findings from one academic health system may not apply to other settings.
